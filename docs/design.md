# Design notes

Details moved out of the source comments, by topic. The outline is in the README ("How it works"); each stage is explained in [how_it_works.md](how_it_works.md).

## Persistent tetrahedral mesh (tet_mesh.hpp)

Each tetrahedron is four particles whose circumsphere holds no other particle; its circumcentre is a Voronoi vertex shared by the four cells, and the Voronoi face between particles i and j is the ring of circumcentres of the tetrahedra around the edge i-j. The mesh is kept from step to step, so a step needs only (1) one circumcentre per tetrahedron a repair created, (2) face geometry from those circumcentres, (3) per-cell face lists, volumes and centroids, and the repair (tet_repair) only where the local Delaunay condition fails.

Memory layout: a tetrahedron's four entries are contiguous (row-major on every backend), so a random access touches one memory sector instead of one per entry. Positions and centres are padded to 32 bytes for the same reason (one sector per point). Geometry rows are likewise contiguous so a random access touches one or two sectors instead of D.

Edge walks: max_ring_length (2³⁰) is a bound on walks around an edge, not a limit on faces. Every walk runs on a checked mesh with consistent adjacency, where a ring closes after at most n − 2 tetrahedra, and the bound costs no memory. It only stops a walk on broken adjacency, whose ring then fails to fit the pool (a Kokkos::abort).

Canonical ring order: every ring is stored from the tetrahedron whose other two particle ids are least, towards the smaller of its two ring neighbours (update_face_rings). The face geometry is then summed in the same order whichever tetrahedron the racy repair left as the face's representative.

Edge representatives: every edge has exactly one representative tetrahedron (a bit in Mesh::edges), so a scan over the bits numbers the faces. Edges without a face (the owner's cell dropped a rounding-level face its neighbour kept) are represented by the lowest-numbered tetrahedron around them (represent_dropped), after the adjacency is known to be consistent. In the wave test with 32768 particles 59 edges had no face (start_test.cpp).

Reproducible circumcentres: sort_corners orders the four corners by particle id, so circumcentres computed from them do not depend on the order a repair left them in. The `rounded` namespace rounds every product, sum and difference on its own (no fused multiply-add), because inlined into different kernels the compiler would otherwise contract a * b + c differently per kernel. Both kernels that compute centres (the screening of a step, tet_repair::move_screened, and the centres of created tetrahedra, circumcentres()) use these pieces on sorted corners, and which kernel computes a given tetrahedron depends on the order of the repair's flips; so the same tetrahedron gets the same bits either way and the face geometry is the same run to run. Slivers are detected by six times the volume against the product of the edge lengths (1e-4 threshold) and their centre is recomputed on the snapped grid, where numerator and denominator are exact integers and the division happens once at the end. Centres are computed from offsets in whole grid steps and stored in grid steps, wrapped into [0, period) and rounded once (store_centre). Stored as box coordinates from a corner's rounded k · step, as before #45, the centres of cospherical tetrahedra differed by an ulp of the coordinate (up to 1e-7 grid steps) whenever the step was not a power of two or the corners sat in different periodic images, and faces that are zero in exact arithmetic got areas of 1e-8 of the cell's surface.

Periodic wrap: the difference to the nearest image multiplies by 1 / side instead of dividing; it picks the same image unless d is within an ulp of half a side.

Face rings between steps (FaceRings): face start(t) + rank is the rank-th edge, in bit order, that tetrahedron t represents; its ring is pool[ring_start(f), ring_start(f) + ring_length(f)). The pool comes twice, one in use and a spare to compact it into. Between updates the spare is the mesh's scratch memory; during update_face_rings and update_cells it holds their temporaries (the last step's numbers, the dirty list, the renumbering table), which cost no memory of their own. When the emptied pool is the smaller one (the spare had been grown as scratch), the rings move back into it, grown only to hold them and half as much to append, and the larger pool stays the spare: both pools at the size of the scratch would be its size twice. Face arrays and pools grow by 1/32 and 1/4.

Face geometry (faces()): fan triangles (first, previous, v) give twice the area vector and the centroid (previous + v) weighted by the triangle's area projected on the owner-to-neighbour vector dj (the face is perpendicular to dj up to rounding), divided once per face; the idea is from Doug Rennehan's plume, commit 6cbb429.

Cell lists (cells()): per-cell face lists in CSR, in increasing id of the other particle, with volume and centroid summed in that order so they are the same bits run to run. In D dimensions a cell's pyramid over a face has volume |A·d| / (2D) and its centroid lies D / (D + 1) of the way from the generator to the face centroid (3D: 1/6 and 3/4). The CUDA sort is a warp per cell with a bitonic sort (lane l holds entry l; more than 32 entries fall back to one thread). The CUDA sums put 32 / lanes cells in a warp, the lanes of a cell gather its list a batch at a time and the sums, in list order, share one instruction stream; they equal the sequential loop of CellLists::sum because a pyramid of zero past the end of the list changes nothing. After a repair the incremental form (3′) only re-sorts cells at a corner of a created tetrahedron; all cells sum again since areas and centroids are new every step.

## Starting tetrahedralization (tet_start.hpp)

The result is exactly the Delaunay tetrahedralization of the snapped positions, ties broken by the symbolic perturbation of exact_predicates, for any input including lattices and other cospherical layouts. The face rings of a clipping build give a fast guess (tet_mesh::from_rings), but rounding decides its near-ties, so neighbouring cells can disagree: holes and overlaps on near-degenerate input, now and then on random input. build() checks the guess exactly and rebuilds the cells around whatever is wrong by the exact per-thread clipping (shell_voronoi::construct): every vertex is named by the three particles whose bisectors meet there and every cut is the exact perturbed in-sphere sign, so cells agree with each other by construction.

Why the exact check suffices: a closed, consistently oriented complex covers the box a whole number of times (the volume says once), and a locally Delaunay triangulation is Delaunay. The check therefore tests per tetrahedron: not flat; across each face a tetrahedron sharing it points back, with its far vertex on the other side and outside this circumsphere; every particle used; volumes sum to the box.

## Start test and mesh check (start_test.cpp, mesh_check.hpp)

start_test checks the start against an independent brute-force host oracle (test only, O(tetrahedra × particles)): (1) a triangulation of the box: no flat tetrahedron, every triangle in exactly two tetrahedra whose apexes lie on opposite sides, adjacency equal to that pairing, every particle used, exact volumes summing to the box; (2) every circumsphere empty of every other particle (exact, perturbed). Both hold only for the Delaunay tetrahedralization, which the perturbation makes unique. The emptiness test scans only the particles whose x lies within the circumradius (sorted by x; brute force otherwise). Boxes: cubes of side 1, 2, 3, a 1 × 1 × 0.125 slab (8 cells across) and a 1 × 2 × 3 box; the slab uses a 64 × 64 × 8 lattice with a degenerate lattice case, fewer cells across would leave cells uncertified (see voronoi_mesh_test); on the 1:2:3 box the sides are not whole numbers of grid steps, so positions are rounded. In box 3 positions are not on the grid; the start is exact on their snapped positions all the same. The oracle is not blind: it rejects the mesh with one corner moved to another particle.

mesh_check: the relative volume error is scaled by the cell's size in grid steps. Positions are doubles near 1, so geometry rounds at about 4 × 10⁻⁸ grid steps whatever the cell; the relative error of a cell h steps wide is about 4 × 10⁻⁸ / h (measured on clumps 512 to 2²⁰ steps wide, contrast.grid_scale). The plain clipping can return a cell of aspect ratio 10⁵ as degenerate with volume 0; the start patches such cells, the reference clipping does not.

## Exact predicates (exact_predicates.hpp, exact_predicates_2d.hpp)

Positions are snapped to a grid of 2³⁰ steps across the longest side of the periodic box. Differences are exact integers, wrapped to the nearest periodic image. Each predicate evaluates its determinant in double together with a bound on the rounding error and decides there when the sign is certain. Otherwise it repeats the evaluation exactly: the orientation in 128-bit integers, the in-sphere determinant in a 256-bit signed sum (exact for coordinates below 2⁴⁰). In-sphere ties are broken by symbolic perturbation, so every caller agrees on one triangulation even for cospherical points. In 2D the orientation is a difference of two products (exact in 64 bits) and the in-circle determinant is three products of a lift and a cross, at most 2¹²² each, exact in 128 bits.

One grid step, the longest side divided by 2³⁰, serves every axis, and each side is a whole number period[a] of steps. The grid is isotropic, so the integer in-sphere test is the in-sphere test of the snapped points themselves. Per-axis steps of 2³⁰ on every side would stretch the axes differently, and the in-sphere test on the stretched integers would not be the one of the points. A side that is not a whole number of steps is rounded to one (relative change below 2⁻³¹ times longest over side; none for sides in ratios of powers of two, such as 2 × 2 × 1/16); side[] holds the rounded sides. A cubic box is 2³⁰ steps on every axis.

The grid step of a coordinate (the function after the Box) is the one definition of "on the grid" that every caller uses: the largest k with k × step ≤ x, the product rounded as the mesh's positions k × step are, so that snapping such a position gives k back. The quotient x / step can round to just below k when the step has a long mantissa. Where the step is a power of two, or k × step is exact, it never does, and in a cube x / step equals x / side × 2³⁰ to the bit, because scaling by 2³⁰ is exact.

The double filter for det3 uses the permanent (the same sum with absolute values) to bound the error: |det − exact| ≤ 8 eps × permanent. The perturbed in-sphere test lifts each point by eps^(its id), lower ids dominating; it is never a tie because the term of the query point is the tetrahedron's own orientation. The cofactor of lift i is (−1)ⁱ times the orientation of the other four points taken in order of increasing particle id (in 2D, −(−1)ⁱ times the orientation of the other three).

## Repair of the persistent mesh (tet_repair.hpp, tet_flips.hpp, repair_test.cpp)

Each device round (tet_repair.hpp) does four things. It finds the shared triangles that fail the local Delaunay test (exact, ties perturbed) among the tetrahedra marked dirty. It plans for each a 2→3 flip (convex pair) or a 3→2 flip (an edge of the triangle with three tetrahedra around it). Every plan then votes, with a hash of its index, on the tetrahedra it would rewrite and their outer neighbours, and the largest vote wins. The plans that won all their votes are applied; their new tetrahedra are dirty for the next round and losing plans retry. Triangles no flip repairs (none is valid, or none closes its cavity) and tetrahedra seen inverted are listed as stuck, for the host fallback (tet_flips::finish) or a rebuild.

The vote is a bijective hash of the plan index (odd multiplier 0x9e3779b1). The work list is in space order on one thread, and with "largest index wins everywhere" a lattice's first step made almost no progress per round. The votes carry the round in the high 32 bits, so a vote of an earlier round always loses and locks never need resetting. A plan is kept between planning and applying in 36 bytes instead of 72. Checking each candidate flip for cavity closure inside one plan() made steps 2 % slower, so the first valid flip is taken and only the others are tried when it does not close.

The host fallback (tet_flips.hpp) works because a shared triangle is locally Delaunay when the far vertex of either tetrahedron lies outside the other's circumsphere. The device repair flips failing triangles while a flip is valid and closes its cavity. The host rebuilds the surroundings of those it left (Bowyer-Watson on the particles around them) and the device flips again. Every decision is an exact sign on the snapped positions shared with the device, so a tie is the same tie everywhere. finish() copies the tetrahedra within a few adjacency steps of the stuck entries to the host as a local mesh with its own particle numbering in the same order (so every exact decision is the same; faces to the rest of the mesh are open), repairs it there, and writes the changed and new tetrahedra back; new ones are appended after state.count so the device free list stays valid. Every changed tetrahedron is touched and listed, each edge around them gets one representative (the lowest-numbered tetrahedron around it; a tetrahedron whose edge bits change is touched too), and the device repair runs again from them and the stuck entries to settle the border and find what the host could not repair. If that still leaves stuck entries the region grows once (8, then 16 steps); what is left is stats.stuck, and voronoi::Mesh then rebuilds. The rebuild of a stuck triangle fails for a particle that shares a grid position with another: no tetrahedron holds both, flips never bring it back, no wider region helps, so it stays stuck and a rebuild refuses the input.

The screening in move_screened lists tetrahedra with a neighbour's far vertex inside or near the circumsphere, and badly shaped ones, in double precision using the circumcentres; the margin covers the snapping of positions to the exact grid (largest move √3 grid steps), and what it lists is decided exactly by repair. Circumcentres are computed with corners sorted by particle id in rounded arithmetic, as tet_mesh::stored_centre does for created tetrahedra, so the bits are the same whichever kernel and corner order computed them.

repair_test.cpp covers: jittered points (ten steps); a lattice and a wave lattice (four steps; their exact starting meshes are decided by the perturbation alone) carried by a smooth flow at 0.03 and at 0.3 spacings per step; icosahedral shells (two steps at 0.03; the first step needs the host's local rebuilds); and jittered points in a box of side 2 (snapping divides by the box), in a 1:2:3 box and in a 1:1:0.125 slab (64 × 64 × 8; the flow is stretched with the box, steps are in spacings). Every step runs the pipeline (screened device flips, host fallback when stuck, face rings and geometry); then the mesh must be Delaunay, its neighbours must equal those of an exact rebuild by clipping, and the volumes from its faces must match the clipped cells. exact_predicates_test.cpp also checks a slab with z period 2²⁵ steps, with the axes rolled (y, z, x), against the copy shifted by half the slab.

## Mesh interface and fallback policy (voronoi_mesh.hpp)

Particles are named by their index in the caller's position array (the particle id); every output uses these ids, and every tie between cospherical particles is broken by them, at the start and in the repair alike. Topology and geometry both come from the snapped positions, so they always agree. Faces are stored once, by the particle with the lower id (the owner), with the area vector from owner to neighbour and the centroid relative to the owner. update() gives faces and cell volumes (summed with atomics: the last bits may differ run to run). update_cells() adds the per-cell face lists, each cell's faces in increasing id of the other particle, and sums volumes and centroids in that order, so they are reproducible bit for bit. Faces are numbered by the tetrahedra that represent them, which follow the particles' order at the start, so faces come nearly sorted by owner when the caller's order is spatial (Morton, say). Hidden: the cell list, the clipping, the tetrahedra and their repair, the face rings. Room is left for later work, neither implemented: ghost (not owned) particles of a multi-GPU run would be particles like the others with ids above the owned ones, and a repair limited to an active region would need a list of the active particles.

Fallback policy when the device repair leaves stuck triangles, measured on an A100 at 2.1M cells: a rebuild takes 0.17–0.5 s (about 0.24 µs per cell); the host fallback takes 0.12 ms per stuck entry on a wave lattice (20.8 s for 178k) but 37–54 ms in a converging flow, where the neighbourhoods are dense and stuck entries come from. So `cheapest` plans for the dear case: the host only up to n / 200000 entries (10 at 2.1M, none below 200000 cells). No clock decides, so the same input takes the same path.

A step of the order of the box leaves tetrahedra whose corners see each other through different images: every local test passes and the mesh covers the torus many times, and their volumes do not add up (the volume check in update()). The update_cells increment (tet_mesh::cells_incremental) is used after few flips; beyond about one tetrahedron created per five cells, rebuilding the lists is as fast (measured at 2.1M). After a refusal there is no mesh and the next update() builds from scratch.

## 1D sorted mesh (sorted_mesh.hpp)

The 1D Voronoi mesh sorts generators by their snapped position, with faces at the midpoints between neighbours and the volume the distance between the faces; the order is decided on the snapped integers with ties by particle id (the 1D symbolic perturbation), so it is exact. The grid is finer than the 3D one: 2⁵² steps across the line (Grid), the resolution of a double position, against 2³⁰. A cell's volume comes from the snapped points and its mass from the fluxes, so a grid step shows as relative density noise step / h: 2⁻¹² at N = 2¹⁸ on 2³⁰ steps, the size of the error of that resolution (research_notes/stage1_1d_2026-11-04.md). 1D needs no bounded integer predicates, so the fine grid costs only the wider key.

A periodic line [0, L) closes the order into a cycle. A line with walls [0, L] puts a mirror of the end generators behind each wall (a generator at x₀ has its mirror at −x₀, and at 2L − x_{n−1}), so the end cells reach the walls exactly. Distances are whole grid steps: gap(s) is the distance from slot s to slot s + 1 (to the mirror behind a wall: twice the distance to the wall), so volumes, (gap(s − 1) + gap(s)) / 2, and centroids relative to the generator, (gap(s) − gap(s − 1)) / 4, are exact multiples of a quarter step, the same bits wherever they are computed. The boundary conditions of walls belong to the hydro.

The Mesh<1> contract is that of Mesh<3> with D = 1: faces once, owner the lower id, area ±1 from owner to neighbour, centroids relative to the owner. On a line with walls the two wall faces have neighbour −1 (at 0) and −2 (at L), area pointing out. Faces are numbered by slot: face s lies between slots s and s + 1 (s < n on a periodic line; n − 1 inner faces on a line with walls, then the wall faces n − 1 at 0 and n at L). The per-cell face lists are cell i's two faces at 2i, 2i + 1, in increasing id of the other particle (a wall's negative id first). update() computes all of it at once, without atomics; update_cells() has nothing left to do. update(x) snaps, then checks whether the order of the last update still holds (one pass; a periodic line may have rotated as particles wrapped around) and sorts again only if particles crossed: a periodic line is still sorted as a cycle with exactly one descent, one with walls with none. order(x) does only that, since the 1D hydro reads the line and needs none of the face arrays. On CUDA the sort is a stable radix sort over the key bits (ids in increasing order keep ties by id); elsewhere std::sort.

## 2D triangulation (tri_mesh.hpp)

The persistent Delaunay triangulation of the generators on the torus has exactly 2n triangles and 3n edges at all times, so slots and face numbers never change: a 2-2 flip rewrites two triangles and one face in place. A Voronoi face has two vertices, the circumcentres of the two triangles of its edge, and needs no ring pool.

Triangles are counter-clockwise with the lowest particle id first (a flip keeps this), so a circumcentre computed from the corners in order is the same bits whatever history led to the triangle. Faces are computed from the side of the lower apex, also independent of history: geometry is a function of the positions alone. The row layout of Triangles is corners 0 to 2 then the apexes across the edge opposite corner k (3 + k); the screening reads only that row. A particle's triangles are those it is the lowest of, in ring order, which is how RingLookup finds a triangle from three particles. Face rows (float, FaceRows) are written when allocated. Faces are numbered by the triangle with the lower apex, in triangle order.

## 2D repair (tri_repair.hpp)

screen(): one pass over the triangles with the new positions writes every circumcentre and lists the edges that fail the in-circle test. The test shares its products with the centre, reads only the corners and apexes, and is filtered in double; each edge is tested from the side of the lower apex. Inverted or flat triangles list their three edges.

repair(): Lawson's 2-2 flips in rounds over the list of edges (one entry is triangle << 2 | corner opposite the edge). An edge between two positive triangles flips when it is not locally Delaunay (exact, perturbed); such a quadrilateral is strictly convex. An edge at an inverted or flat triangle flips when both new triangles are positive, which removes the inversion. Conflicting flips are settled by votes: a planned flip writes a hash of the edge and the round into the locks of its two triangles and four outer neighbours (fetch-max), and it applies if it holds all six. Keys grow with the round, so locks never need resetting, and the set of winners does not depend on the order of the list. The highest key always wins, so every round makes progress: flips of positive edges lower the lifted surface and flips at inversions lower their number. A flip rewrites its two triangles and one face in place, patches the outer neighbours and lists the four outer edges; losers stay listed.

What can remain are inversions no flip of this kind removes (stuck): a point moved past more than its neighbouring triangle. tri_finish::finish() then replaces a patch around them on the host, or voronoi::Mesh rebuilds.

The signed-area total (twice the sum, in grid steps²) guards against a step of order the box, which can leave a mesh with every local test passing that covers the torus several times; its areas do not add up to the box. A flip of two positive triangles keeps the sum, so without an inversion the screen's sum holds after the repair, and the area kernel runs only after an inversion.

## 2D host fallback (tri_finish.hpp)

Triangles stuck inverted are replaced, with a patch of triangles around them, by the Delaunay triangles of the patch's own corners (Bowyer-Watson in a local frame, exact and perturbed by particle id). The result is accepted if it has the patch's boundary edge for edge and as many triangles; else the patch grows. The patch keeps its slots and face numbers. Its boundary edges go back to the device repair, which settles what the patch changed around it. Cost O(k²) in the patch size k, a few dozen triangles.

## 2D polygon clipping (polygon_clip.hpp, voronoi_mesh_2d.hpp)

The cells are built by clipping a polygon with the neighbours, in shell_voronoi's walk in 2D: grid cells around the generator nearest first, on the level fitted to the local density, until nothing within twice the farthest vertex is left. A cell is a convex polygon: a cycle of edges, each the bisector with one neighbour, and vertices between consecutive edges. A bisector cuts off one contiguous arc of vertices, which two new vertices replace. Every decision is exact: whether a vertex lies beyond a bisector is the perturbed in-circle test of the generator, the vertex's two neighbours and the candidate (exact_predicates_2d.hpp), which a double comparison with a rounding bound settles nearly always. So the cells agree with each other and there is one kind of cell, no rounded pass and no patching.

The 2D mesh (voronoi_mesh_2d.hpp) has Mesh<3>'s contract: particles on one grid position are refused by name, a refused update leaves no mesh and the next builds, no particles are an invalid_argument that leaves the object as it was, and a repair whose areas do not fill the box once is rebuilt. Faces are numbered for good at the start: there are always exactly 3n, and a flip gives its face new particles. volume() is current after update_cells() only (no atomic sums in update()). The cell list, the rings and the triangles are hidden members. The host fallback runs when at most n / 4000 triangles are stuck.

Mesh<1>::insert() and remove() change the line between updates without a sort: a binary search per request and one pass over the line. For request r of a change, `Changes` gives id(r), the new id or the id removed (-1: deferred, nothing changed for it); neighbour(r, 0 and 1), the particles left and right of it, or the wall (-1 at 0, -2 at L), by ids of before the change; overlap(r, 0 and 1), for an insertion the length the new cell takes from those two old cells, for a removal the length each of them takes from the removed cell, summing to that cell's volume either way; and volume(r, 0 and 1), those cells' volumes before the change. An insertion is exact against the old cells, so a caller holding conserved quantities moves the fraction overlap / volume of each neighbour's values of before the change (a cell can give to both sides) and conserves them. New ids are first, first + 1, ... in request order. remove() keeps ids dense: the last ids fill the holes, and after moving its data by neighbour and overlap the caller copies id moved(m, 0) to id moved(m, 1), m < moves, positions too. A rename can leave ties out of id order, which the next update sorts. Per-particle arrays grow by a quarter when full.

## Tests of the 2D and 1D meshes (mesh2d_test.cpp, mesh1d_test.cpp, mesh_test.cpp, clipping_test.cpp, distortion_test.cpp)

mesh2d_test checks predicates (the in-circle sign against a 4 x 4 integer determinant, and the perturbed test consistent under every permutation of its points) and the mesh on random, jittered, clustered, lattice, hexagonal and wave inputs in square and non-square boxes: (a) brute force over all particles, every triangle positive, every edge shared by two triangles, signed areas summing to the box in exact integers, no particle strictly inside a circumcircle; (b) every cell clipped in long double by the bisectors of all particles in all images, comparing volumes, centroids, the neighbours across faces of nonzero length, and their length and centroid; (c) the face lists in order of the other id. Moving: after steps of 0.03 to 1 spacings the repaired mesh equals a mesh built from nothing bit for bit (volumes, centroids, faces), and repeated runs give the same hash. Refusals: coincident particles at the start and in a step, by name; no mesh after a refusal and the next update builds; no particles; a mapping of degree 4 and kicks of order the box. Sanitizers cost about 11 times, so the longest runs shrink there with the same code paths.

mesh1d_test: the order is the sort of the snapped positions with ties by id; volumes and centroids are the exact midpoint cells (quarter grid steps); the volumes sum to the line exactly; faces are stored once with owner < neighbour, area ±1 from owner to neighbour and centroid relative to the owner; the per-cell face lists name a cell's two faces in increasing id of the other particle; on a line with walls the end cells reach the walls and the two wall faces point out. After every step (small steps that keep the order, large ones that cross, points wrapping through L) the mesh equals a fresh build bit for bit, and the order is sorted again only when points crossed.

mesh1d_test on insertion and removal: over random batches, on a periodic line that has rotated and on one with walls, near the walls, on generators and twice in one gap, the mesh equals the oracle and a fresh build, the reported overlaps equal the overlaps of the old and new cells computed on the host (integers, brute force), a transfer by them conserves, and the arrays grow.

mesh_test: from_rings yields the same tetrahedra as a host construction from the rings with consistent adjacency and one representative per edge; the Voronoi geometry from their circumcentres (on the stored rings, and on rings walked from the edge representatives) matches the clipped cells and tet_start::check passes. A sliver is three corners on a circle in a grid plane and a fourth one grid step above a point near that circle; its circumcentre in grid units is (A / 2, B / 2, (C² + D² + 1 − A C − B D) / 2) from corner 0.

clipping_test: besides the cases in the file head, the exact per-thread kernel (the start's patch) must give the same cells, as must a base grid twice as coarse. Non-finite positions (NaN, infinity) never fall below the reach, so every other cell is the cell without them. The idea is Doug Rennehan's, PR #2.

distortion_test: prescribed flows without hydro or regularisation, then with a drift to the centroids in the driver (the library layer has none).


## Shell Voronoi construction (shell_voronoi.hpp)

Each cell is built by walking grid cells around its generator, nearest first, and clipping with every particle that can still cut it. A particle at distance d cuts the cell only if d < 2R, where R is the largest distance from the generator to a current vertex; the walk stops at the first grid cell lying entirely beyond 2R, which certifies the cell. The grid level fits the local density (see Stencil). There is no candidate table: particles are clipped nearest-first from a small private buffer.

Output is lean: every cell stores its volume and centroid, and each face is stored once, by the cell with the lower particle id (its owner), as face geometry or as a face ring (Cells). Slots, positions and cell offsets come from a built neighbor_search::CellList.

Certification holds within one periodic image only: the reach 2R must stay below 3/8 of the shortest side (reach 3 at 8 cells across), otherwise the cell is uncertified (too few particles across a thin slab). voronoi::Mesh refuses such a box. The walk reach is max_reach, or less where 2 reach + 1 cells along the shortest axis (`fewest` tiles) would visit a cell twice. The starting cube must lie well inside half the shortest side so that every candidate that can cut the cell is unique in the periodic image.

When the stencil of a level ends before the cell is certified, the walk continues one level coarser and skips the grid cells the finer walk covered.

### Face and ring pools

The face pool has one counter; the warp kernel claims blocks of many cells' faces at a time. The ring pool is split into equal chunks; cell t claims its block in chunk pool_chunk(t), with one counter per chunk, because a single counter serializes the build when every cell claims. The chunk is chosen by a multiplicative hash, as slot order is spatial and t % chunks could pick, say, only the cells at one box face. Cells whose chunk is full are face_overflow; the construct functions then build again with a pool of needed() entries (every chunk as large as the fullest).

### Cell types

RoundedCell and ExactCell share the walk, the cut topology and the face walk of construct_pass. Both keep planes (each with the particle id beyond it; box planes are virtual particles from n on) and vertices, each listing its three planes in the order that walks every face the same way round and the three vertices across its edges (adjacent[v][k] lies across the edge from corner k to corner k + 1). A cut finds its hole and a face walk its next vertex through those links; vertices stay in their slots, removed ones are marked and their slots reused (an idea from Doug Rennehan's plume). Planes and Vertices are capacities: the defaults fit almost every cell, the rare larger ones are built again with big ones, and the exact cells that overflow those on the host with huge ones.

ExactCell works in grid steps relative to the snapped generator. Vertex v is the circumcentre of the generator and the particles of its three planes, -w / (2 o); a particle at e lies inside that sphere iff e.w + |e|² o has the opposite sign of o (w and o are exact integers, stored rounded once), decided by a filter and, when that is uncertain, by the exact perturbed in-sphere sign. Neighbouring cells agree by construction: these are the cells of the unique perturbed Delaunay tetrahedralization. The start's patch is in tet_start.

## Warp kernel (shell_voronoi_warp.hpp)

One warp builds one cell. The polyhedron lives in the warp's slice of shared memory; lane l owns vertices l and l + 32 and planes l and l + 32. Every vertex stores its three planes and the three vertices across its edges, so a cut, the creation of new vertices and the walk around a face are each constant work per vertex, done by all lanes at once. Particles of a grid ring are tested 32 at a time and clipped nearest-first after a warp sort. Teams of 16 or 8 lanes per cell were 2× and 3.6× slower on an A100.

Four cells (warps) per block put their polyhedra (4.4 KB each) in 18 KB of block scratch. Capping registers so that 5 blocks fit per SM is about 4 % faster on an A100. Plane normals only weight face centroids: a rounded normal scales every triangle of a face alike, so float is enough. The owned-face pool is claimed face_block entries per warp so the pool counter sees one atomic per block instead of one per cell.

Persistent warps (about two waves of resident blocks) take cells from a counter until none are left, so a slow cell holds up only its own warp; this was 1.06× on uniform random points over one block per four cells. Near-uniform input (every particle at the base level) takes the kernel without level changes and fine-cell searches; its smaller code is 4 % faster on uniform random points. The pyramid over a face from the generator has volume A.first / 3 and its centroid lies at 3/4 of the face centroid (idea from Doug Rennehan's plume, commit 6cbb429).

## Cell list (neighbor_search.hpp)

Particles of a periodic box are sorted along a Morton curve: an implicit octree in which every node, from a tile down to cells 2^-(levels + fine_levels) tiles wide, owns a contiguous range of slots.

Tiles: the box is cut into tiles[0] × tiles[1] × tiles[2] near-cubes of about its shortest side (one tile, the box itself, for a cube), numbered in row-major order; each is the root of an octree, and a cell number is the tile's number above the Morton number inside it. So unequal sides need no padding to a cube (a 2 × 2 × 1/16 slab is 32 × 32 × 1 tiles).

Slot q holds particle order(q) at positions(q). The base grid (2^levels cells per tile side) has a dense offset table: base cell c owns the slots [cell_offsets(c), cell_offsets(c + 1)), so a block of 8^k base cells at a coarser level owns a contiguous range too. Below the base grid, the slots of a base cell are sorted by fine(q), the Morton key of the particle inside its base cell (fine_levels levels), so a finer node is found by binary search. Ties keep particle-id order, which makes the slot order deterministic.

level(q) is the grid level whose cells hold about one particle near slot q: the deepest node around q with at least 64 particles gives the local density. Clipping walks the cells of that level around each particle, so clusters and voids each get a fitting cell size. Buffers persist across builds; a build allocates nothing. The sort works in the storage of `positions` (24 bytes per particle), which is only written after it: two buffers of 64-bit keys, two of particle ids.

## Benchmark drivers (voronoi_bench.cpp, mesh2d_bench.cpp)

voronoi_bench rebuild <n> <distribution> [repeats=5] [--no-check]: full rebuild by the warp clipping (the per-thread kernel on CPU backends and for overflow cells). The cells are checked (support::check_cells, PASS/FAIL; brute force on a sample, seconds at 2.1M) unless --no-check.

voronoi_bench step <n> <distribution> <displacement> [steps=10] [--verify]: the persistent mesh (voronoi::Mesh): the start kernels (clipping with rings, from_rings, tet_start::build) and the mesh's start, then `steps` steps of the flow, `displacement` spacings each: device repair (screened), the fallback when stuck (the cheaper of host and rebuild, voronoi::Fallback::cheapest), circumcentres, face rings, faces and cells. --verify compares every step with an exact rebuild (support::compare_with_rebuild).

Other flags: --memory (both modes) reports device memory per phase (Kokkos allocations: current, peak, and the live allocations at the peak by label). --kernels (step) gives time per kernel label, mean over the steps after the first (run with CUDA_LAUNCH_BLOCKING=1 on CUDA, else launches only). --checksum (step) gives a per-step checksum of the volume and centroid bits. --box=<Lx>,<Ly>,<Lz> (both modes; default 1,1,1) sets a periodic box; lattices get about equal spacings along every axis (e.g. --box=2,2,0.0625 with 4194304 jittered points is a 512 × 512 × 16 slab), the other distributions stretch with the box.

Distributions: uniform, jittered, lattice, wave, clustered, halo, or <sc|bcc|fcc|shells|planes|pairs>@<perturbation in spacings>.

mesh2d_bench <jittered|random|clustered|lattice> <n> [--steps=<k>] [--move=<spacings per step>] [--repeats=<r>] [--box=<lx>x<ly>] [--unsorted] prints M cells/s of the build (cell list, clipping, triangles, faces, cell lists) and of the steps (repair, centres and faces, cell lists), flips, rounds, the steps that fell back, and the peak of device allocations in bytes per cell. With --move every step the points drift along a smooth flow, as in voronoi_bench (a sum of sines), so that shear and compression both occur. Particles are numbered along a Morton curve (--unsorted: as generated).

## voronoi::Mesh contract test (voronoi_mesh_test.cpp)

On positions off the snapping grid in a box of side 2: the first update builds the mesh, later ones repair it, and after each the faces are those of an exact rebuild (neighbours, volumes); on near-cospherical shells, where the device repair leaves stuck triangles, Fallback::host (the host finishes them) and Fallback::rebuild (the step rebuilds) are exact too. Faces are stored once, owner < neighbour, and close every cell; volumes fill the box; cell centroids equal those of clipping. The per-cell face lists (update_cells) name each cell's faces once, in increasing id of the other particle. Particle ids are the caller's: a permuted input gives the permuted mesh and the same input the same bits. A local rebuild by clipping returns each face of the listed cells once, equal to the mesh's. The scratch memory can be used between updates. For a box of three sides, volumes fill it, box() refuses it, and a slab too thin for its particles is refused with a clear error.

## Test oracle (src/test/oracle.hpp)

The oracle shares no code with the library. It uses its own periodic differences, exact orientation and in-sphere signs in plain 128-bit arithmetic, and Voronoi cells by half-space clipping against every generator in every periodic image, in long double. It takes data only (integer grid positions, tetrahedra, faces) and is brute force, for tests only.

A mesh is accepted as the Delaunay tetrahedralization on the torus when three conditions hold. First, every corner of a tetrahedron is its own nearest image from every other corner; the library stores no images, so a tetrahedron wider than half a period has no meaning and is reported as `ambiguous`. Second, the tetrahedra cover the torus: positively oriented, each triangle twice, adjacency equal to that pairing, every particle used, exact volumes summing to the box. Third, no image of any particle lies strictly inside a circumsphere. Points on a sphere are allowed in this check; the tie-break is compared separately against a fresh build of the same positions (different_tetrahedra).

The sign of s·t over four terms with |s| < 2⁶⁶ and |t| < 2⁹⁸ splits each t at 2⁴⁸ so both partial sums fit 128 bits. The in-sphere test uses the circumcentre C = N / (2 det): e is inside iff |e|² < 2 e·C. Half the box diagonal is a sphere radius that covers the torus, so an empty sphere is no larger.

## Grid positions (src/test/support.hpp)

The mesh decides topology on positions snapped to the grid of the exact predicates (2³⁰ steps across the box) and computes geometry from the positions themselves. Off the grid the two disagree on near-degenerate cells (volumes about 10⁻⁹ apart) and on particles closer than about 10³ grid steps, so every test input and every flow step is kept on the grid (to_grid).

## Test inputs (src/test/support.hpp)

Jittered points are a lattice moved by up to 0.1 spacings; wave is a lattice moved by 10⁻⁵ spacings. The clustered input is eight Gaussian blobs over a uniform background, a density ratio of about 1:100. The halo input is four halos of density 1 / (r² + c²) out to r = 0.2 with c = 0.002 (core to edge 1:10⁴) over a 10 % uniform background, about 1:10⁵ from the voids to the halo cores; the enclosed mass m(r) = r − c atan(r / c) is inverted by bisection. Degenerate layouts: sc, bcc and fcc lattices have 8, 4 and 6 cells meeting at vertices; shells are icosahedral shells of 12 points without a centre point, so 12 cells meet at each shell centre.

## Clipping checks (src/test/support.hpp)

The brute-force generator-distance check on a sample of cells costs about 2.5×10⁸ distances in all, so the sample size is 2.5×10⁸ / n, clamped to [16, n]. Clipping drops faces below rounding level while the mesh keeps every Delaunay edge, hence "extra" faces are tolerated below an area of 10⁻⁹ spacings² and volumes are compared to 10⁻¹⁰.

## Distortion driver (src/test/distortion.hpp)

Fields run on the device; 256³ is fine. The turbulence field is random-phase solenoidal modes with k^(−5/6) amplitudes and drifting phases. The collapse and noh fields converge within radius 0.16 (Sedov and Noh reversed); pancake collapses a layer of half thickness 0.16 onto a plane. The aspect ratio is the largest over cells of the reach R over half the distance to the nearest neighbour; reach is the largest 2R of any cell over the box side, and 3/8 is the limit of a build. The drift option is regularisation in this driver only (the library has none).

## Cells under construction (`shell_cell.hpp`)

Rounded and exact cells are shared by the walk, the cut topology and the face walk of `construct_pass`. Both keep planes, with the particle id beyond each (box planes are virtual particles from n on), and vertices, each listing its three planes in face-walking order and the three vertices across its edges: `adjacent[v][k]` lies across the edge from corner k to k + 1. Removed vertices are marked and their slots reused. `RoundedCell` is the portable kernel and the warp kernel's CUDA fallback.

## Construction figures (scripts/construction_figures.py)

`scripts/construction_figures.py` draws `docs/figures/construction_*.png` with LUNA's `figstyle.py`. Nothing in them is sketched by hand. The geometry comes from Python ports of the library's 2D code in exact integers: the cell list (`neighbor_search::build`, D = 2), the walk and clip of `polygon_clip::construct_pass`, the perturbed `in_circle` of `exact_predicates_2d.hpp`, and the Lawson rounds of `tri_repair` with their six-lock votes. Checks: the clipped cell's area against `scipy.spatial.Voronoi`, the repaired triangulation against `scipy.spatial.Delaunay`, the face of the 3D panel against the bisector plane, and the tie-break against every argument order. On 2026-11-09 the ports gave the same slot order, levels and rings as the C++ headers on a uniform set of 120 points and a clumped set of 800 (a throwaway driver, not kept). The point sets are relaxed random sets (Lloyd sweeps) and a clumped set; the moved mesh is tiled 3 × 3, so its periodic box sees no hull, and its moved positions are kept unwrapped, as the code takes every difference to the nearest image. The figure of four cocircular points scales a lattice circle of radius 5 by an odd factor onto the 2³⁰ grid, chosen so that the rounded in-circle values disagree in sign.
