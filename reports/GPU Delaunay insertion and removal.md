# Split by flips, merge by rebuild

The best exact GPU design for refinement in a persistent Delaunay mesh follows from one asymmetry. **Insertion fits the flip machinery you already have. Removal does not.** In 2D and 3D, insert each new generator by locating it in its parent's star, applying a 1-3 (2D) or 1-4 (3D) split, and handing the new facets to the existing vote-locked Lawson or 2-3/3-2 flip repair. This is gDel2D/gDel3D's Phase 1 without the from-scratch scaffolding. A single insertion into a Delaunay mesh followed by flipping never gets stuck, even in 3D. Remove a generator by first selecting an independent set of victims, then building the small Delaunay triangulation of each victim's link under the *same* index-keyed symbolic perturbation as the global mesh, and sewing it into the hole. This is CGAL's "triangulate and sew", and it is the method your host fallback already resembles. 3D removal by flips can get stuck and needs this rebuild as a fallback anyway. 1D needs no Delaunay machinery at all. The conservative remap comes almost free. Removing a generator only enlarges its neighbours, so each neighbour's share of the dead cell is exactly its volume increase, and the split shares are the two daughter volumes. AREPO's auxiliary meshes and polyhedron clipping are therefore unnecessary in a persistent-mesh code.

No published GPU code reports incremental insertion or removal throughput. The best published exact GPU figure is gDel3D's full rebuild at **about 4.7 M points/s on an H200**, and the best published removal figures are single-core CPU: **0.3–0.6 M/s in 2D and about 0.01 M/s in 3D (CGAL)**. Reaching about 10 M operations/s in 3D, which is what a 1% per-step rate needs to cost under 5% of your current step, therefore goes beyond what anyone has measured. It is plausible because the work per operation is small and local, but it has to be measured.

## The budget: 1% per step needs roughly 5–11 M operations/s

The target sets the bar. Your moving-mesh hydro step currently sustains about 54 M cell updates/s (project measurement). If 1% of cells are split or merged every step, the (de)refinement path must run at **about 10.8 M operations/s to add 5% to the step, or 5.4 M operations/s to add 10%**. At 2.1M cells a 1% batch is about 21,000 operations. Kernel-launch latency (about 5–10 µs per launch, tens of launches) therefore costs a fraction of a millisecond, against a step of about 40 ms. The real danger is not the bulk work but the long tail: rounds in which only a few conflicting or stuck points remain, each paying a full kernel sequence. Both Marot's parallel insertion and gQM3D report exactly this tail ([Marot et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08831v3); [Chen & Tan 2019](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gqm3d_files/gqm3d.pdf)).

The physics allows a clean way out of the tail. AREPO already tolerates cells that stay above or below threshold for a few steps: neighbours are never derefined together, so "some cells sit below m_target/2 for a few time steps", and some cells temporarily exceed 2 m_target because irregular cells are not split ([Vogelsberger et al. 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.1281)). A GPU code can therefore give (de)refinement a **fixed round budget per step and defer the losers to the next step**. That turns an unbounded tail into a bounded cost, with no loss of correctness, because the mesh is exactly Delaunay after every committed operation. The rate also matters in practice. Cosmological AREPO runs invoke refinement "only rarely" ([Vogelsberger et al. 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.1281)), but in the 3D Noh test the low-resolution run created **812,620 cells and destroyed 225,209** from about 4,700 initial cells ([Springel 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.4107)). Compressive problems can thus generate on the order of 100 split events per initial cell over a run, so this path cannot be treated as a rare slow path.

The published throughput landscape below shows that nothing measured matches this bar for incremental operations. It also shows how far CPU removal lags behind insertion.

Three gaps stand out: **no GPU Delaunay vertex removal, no incremental GPU insertion rate, and no absolute modern 2D GPU figure appear anywhere in the literature surveyed**. Radiant Foam maintains a 3D Delaunay mesh incrementally on the GPU during training. Its authors call the update "still computationally expensive" but publish no rate, and used as a full builder their code is about 100x slower than gDel3D ([Govindarajan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.01157); [Taveira et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06408v1)). gDel3D's 40x collapse on clustered input is a warning for refinement, which by nature concentrates new points.

## Flip insertion reuses your engine; one point at a time never gets stuck

gDel3D builds its triangulation in rounds. In each round, every tetrahedron containing uninserted points inserts one of them with a plain 1-4 split ("splitting the tetrahedron containing the point, without any further modification"), and then all flippable non-Delaunay facets are flipped in parallel. Inserting the point nearest each tet's circumcenter cuts failed vertices by more than 2x and flips by 20%. Interleaving insertion and flipping, instead of inserting everything first, cuts failures by nearly two orders of magnitude ([Cao et al. 2014](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/gDel3D.pdf)). 2D flipping never gets stuck, so gDel2D needs only this phase. 3D flipping does get stuck when many points enter at once ("flipping gets stuck long before reaching the DT"), so gDel3D repairs the remaining failed vertices with adaptive star splaying on the CPU. This touches fewer than about 700 stars per 5×10^5 uniform points ([Cao et al. 2014](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/gDel3D.pdf); [Shewchuk 2005](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/papers/star.pdf)). The key theoretical point is Joe's 1991 result, quoted in Nanjappa's thesis: flipping to Delaunay in 3D "works ... only with incremental insertion", that is, one point inserted into a Delaunay triangulation and then flipped ([Nanjappa thesis](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/AshwinNanjappaThesis.pdf)).

Bowyer-Watson is the alternative. Each 3D insertion deletes about 20 tets, creates about 26 and costs about 46 inSphere tests. Rebuilding the cavity boundary and its adjacency takes roughly a third of the time even in HXT, the fastest CPU implementation ([Marot et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08831v3)). gQM3D shows Bowyer-Watson working on a GPU mesh that already exists. Each candidate atomically writes its priority into every tet it wants, then checks after a barrier whether it still holds all of them, and losers abort for the round ([Chen & Tan 2019](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gqm3d_files/gqm3d.pdf)). gDel3D's authors give the counter-argument for the GPU: "locking becomes very inefficient on the GPU, while conflicts during point insertions become uncontrollable", whereas flipping is "very amenable to parallel processing" ([Cao et al. 2014](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/gDel3D.pdf)). Bowyer-Watson's variable-size cavities also hurt warp load balance. For a code that already has an exact, vote-locked parallel flip kernel, flip insertion is the least new code and the best fit to the hardware. No source compares GPU cost per insertion for the two methods head to head.

What makes the batch safe is how the claims are made, not the insertion primitive. gQM3D gives the precise condition: two Delaunay-dependent points inserted into cavities that share only a facet produce a non-Delaunay facet, so concurrent cavities must be **strictly disjoint, boundaries included**, or a flip repair must follow ([Chen & Tan 2019](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gqm3d_files/gqm3d.pdf)). Deterministic reservations, in which each point write-mins its ID onto everything it touches and commits only if it still holds all of it, make the result independent of thread schedule ([Shun, MIT 6.886](https://jshun.csail.mit.edu/6886-s19/lectures/lecture10-2.pdf)). That property matches your bit-reproducibility requirement and maps directly onto vote-based locking.

Our inference for refinement is that the claim should cover the parent's star plus the tets around the split point, keyed on a hash of the particle ID. At 1% density, with about 20 tets per cavity in a mesh of about 6.5N tets, only about 3% of tets are claimed, so one or two rounds should place nearly every point. Each claimed point then flips essentially as a sequential insertion. That puts it inside Joe's guarantee, and stuck facets should all but vanish. This is an estimate; nobody has measured stuck rates for 1% batches into an existing Delaunay mesh. Your existing local Bowyer-Watson fallback covers the remainder. Siblings of one parent are mutually dependent, so a parent should place at most one child per round.

Point location is nearly free for refinement. AREPO places the daughter **0.025 r_cell** from the parent in a random direction ([Weinberger et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.04667)), and GIZMO places its two halves min(h/8, r_near/3) apart, perpendicular to the density gradient ([Hopkins 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7395)). A point that close to its parent lies inside a tet incident to the parent. A few orient3d tests over the parent's roughly 25-tet star find it, with no walk and no history DAG. Even HXT's sorted CPU walk took only 8% of insertion time ([Marot et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08831v3)). Springel notes that such a split "will leave all surrounding Voronoi cells unchanged" ([Springel 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.4107)). The flip sequence is therefore confined to the parent's star, and we expect it to be short: about 10–20 flips in the worst case, fewer for a near-parent point. This is an estimate with no published count.

One trap concerns placement. Exact symmetric offsets on integer coordinates create genuine cospherical configurations. Symbolic perturbation resolves them, but the exact path then dominates run time. On grid-aligned LiDAR data gDel3D became much slower and, in one unverified third-party benchmark, wrong ([DelaunayBench](https://github.com/omarbesbes/DelaunayBench)). A random offset direction, as AREPO uses, avoids systematic degeneracy.

## Removal needs a local rebuild under the same perturbation

2D removal is solved and cheap. Devillers' ear queue fills the hole in O(k log k) by ordering ears by the power of the removed point. It is asymptotically optimal among ear methods but loses to plain flipping below degree about 9. Flipping the degree down to 3 needs at most (k−2)(k−3)/2 incircle tests, and the mean degree is 6 ([Devillers 1999](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/9907023)). Hard-wiring degrees 3–7 alone halves CGAL's removal time ("asymptotic complexity is pointless") ([Devillers 2011, HAL](https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/INRIA/inria-00433107)). Because 2D Lawson flipping never gets stuck, flip-down removal in 2D needs no fallback. It is the 2D removal to use, and it runs as one thread per victim in your flip engine.

3D removal is a different problem. CGAL's 3D deletion costs **about 100 µs per vertex, roughly 12–13x a sorted insertion**, at every size from 100K to 10M points ([Devillers 2012](https://homepages.loria.fr/ODevillers/EuroCG2012/EuroCG12-devillers.pdf)). Removing 1% of 10^7 points would thus take about 10 s on one CPU core, which rules out any host path at scale. The 2013 ESA survey by Buchin, Devillers, Mulzer, Schrijvers and Shewchuk weighs the options. The flip algorithm "does not always work; it can get stuck in a non-Delaunay triangulation from which no flip can make further progress." The ear queue needs a non-standard power-comparison predicate that is "more difficult to make robust". Triangulate-and-sew runs in expected O(d log d + C(P)) and "uses less complicated numerical predicates". On bounded-degree inputs every method ran at similar speed ([Buchin et al. 2013](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/mulzer/pubs/dtDel3DESA.pdf)). Their refinement builds only the part of DT(link) that conflicts with the removed vertex, and they identify that restriction as most of their speedup.

Ledoux, Gold and Baciu do make 3D flip removal robust, ending in a 4-1 flip, but they need two dedicated robustness mechanisms to get there ([Ledoux et al. 2005](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/11424758_76)). Shewchuk's advice applies directly: start with flips, and fall back to star splaying or a rebuild when they stall ([Shewchuk 2005](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/papers/star.pdf)). An exact 3D flip-down path would carry the rebuild with it anyway, so the rebuild should be the primary method.

Devillers and Teillaud name the reason 3D removal "remains a practical problem". When the link vertices are cospherical, DT(link) is not unique, and an arbitrary choice inside the hole may not match the boundary facets already fixed by the outer mesh. Their fix, adopted by CGAL from version 2.3 on, is a symbolic perturbation that is consistent between the hole and the outside ([CGAL Triangulation_3 manual](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Triangulation_3/index.html); [Ledoux et al. 2005](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/11424758_76)). Your code already meets this condition, with one requirement. If the perturbation is keyed to a fixed per-particle index, which your decision to key the mesh by particle ID already implies, then the perturbed Delaunay triangulation is a unique function of the point set. The conflict part of DT(link) then matches the boundary by construction. Perturbation keyed to insertion order would break this, as would a different convention for the lifted coordinate in the local rebuild. This argument is our inference from the cited problem statement.

Kinetic codes offer a third route that reuses flips. Schaller and Meyer-Hermann move the victim toward its nearest neighbour with flip repairs, then collapse the edge. They report deletion "more efficient than insertion" because no location is needed ([Schaller & Meyer-Hermann 2004](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/physics/0302018)). Springel considered and rejected this "move together then merge" approach for hydro, because the decision spans two cells and several steps while the criteria keep changing ([Springel 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.4107)). It also still needs an exact degenerate edge-collapse kernel.

The GPU design that follows has three steps, and every one is our inference, since no published GPU vertex removal exists. First, choose victims as a priority-ordered independent set with a one-ring buffer, as AREPO does. Second, gather each victim's link (about 15 vertices and 27 star tets for Poisson points) into registers or shared memory, and build only its conflict tets with the exact SoS predicate ([Devillers 1999](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/9907023)). Third, sew the result in. A few hundred exact insphere tests per removal, at GPU filtered-predicate rates, suggests **1–10 M removals/s**, two to three orders of magnitude above a CGAL core. That range is an order-of-magnitude estimate and must be measured.

## Moving-mesh codes split near the parent, merge into neighbours, and rebuild

AREPO is the reference design that RICH and others copy ([Yalinewich et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3219)). It refines a cell with m > 2 m_target and derefines one with m < 0.5 m_target, a factor-4 hysteresis band ([Weinberger et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.04667)). Only "roundish" cells are split (maximum face angle below 3.38), because splitting irregular, just-split cells introduces "large local errors" ([Vogelsberger et al. 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.1281)). Derefinement candidates are sorted by priority, any cell with an already-flagged neighbour is skipped, and every neighbour that receives mass is barred from refining in that step. The dead cell's mass, momentum and energy go to its neighbours in proportion to the volume each claims. That volume is computed by clipping the dead polyhedron with neighbour bisectors on an auxiliary local mesh. The split uses the two daughter volumes from another auxiliary mesh. All of this runs after the first half-step fluxes and before the next `create_mesh()`, so **AREPO never inserts into or deletes from a persistent Delaunay mesh**. It rebuilds the active mesh, and the public code has no 1D (de)refinement ([AREPO public source](https://gitlab.mpcdf.mpg.de/vrs/arepo)). Curtis and Sijacki found that narrowing the hysteresis corridor makes cells cycle between refinement and derefinement at high cost ([Curtis & Sijacki 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03445)). GIZMO's mass-threshold split and merge are, in Hopkins' words, "noisy and diffusive", recommended only "if absolutely necessary", yet FIRE-type MFV discs cannot run without them ([Hopkins 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7395)).

A persistent exact mesh changes how the remap is done. Removing a generator only enlarges the cells of its Delaunay neighbours; no cell loses volume. The volume neighbour q claims from the dead cell is therefore exactly V_q(after) − V_q(before), and these increments sum to the dead cell's volume. Likewise, a split changes only the parent cell, which divides into the two daughters ([Springel 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.4107)). Your mesh already recomputes the volumes of touched cells. Recording old volumes for the one-ring before the operation therefore gives AREPO's exact first-order remap weights with **no polyhedron intersection at all** (our inference). This is cheaper than r3d-style clipping, which costs about 0.2–0.8 µs per clip per 2015 CPU core and is the default kernel in LANL's Portage remap framework ([Powell & Abel 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.4941); [Portage SC17](https://sc17.supercomputing.org/SC17%20Archive/tech_poster/tech_poster_pages/post169.html)). The ALE literature solves a harder problem. ReALE keeps the number of generators fixed and remaps by cell intersection or swept faces, and the hybrid schemes reserve intersection for material interfaces because it is expensive ([Loubère et al. 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002199911000121X); [Kucharik & Shashkov 2012](https://cnls.lanl.gov/~shashkov/papers/hybrid_milan.pdf)). Gaburro and co-workers remove the remap altogether. They integrate over space-time control volumes and fill topology changes with degenerate "sliver" elements in 2D (2020), extended in 2026 to 2-3, 3-2 and 4-4 flips in 3D ([Gaburro et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00967); [Gaburro et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23506)). That is the route to take if second-order conservation across (de)refinement events ever matters more than AREPO's first-order transfer.

Two caveats limit the volume-difference shortcut. It requires the (de)refinement to happen at fixed generator positions, before or after mesh motion and not during it. It is also first order: AREPO's public split scales by volume ratio only, and gradient-weighted transfer is an option Springel mentions but the public code does not implement ([AREPO public source](https://gitlab.mpcdf.mpg.de/vrs/arepo)). The daughter inherits the parent's time bin. Mesh regularisation separates the split pair "over the course of a few timesteps" ([Springel 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.4107)).

## Recommended method per dimension

| | Insertion (refine) | Removal (derefine) | Conflict handling | Remap | Expected cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1D** | Split the interval: new site between parent and its neighbour. Neighbour links update by prefix-sum compaction or an in-place splice. | Merge the interval into its two neighbours. | No two adjacent operations per step. | Exact interval lengths. | Negligible; a scan over N or less. |
| **2D** | Locate in parent's star; 1-3 split (2-4 on an edge); Lawson flips in the existing kernel. Never gets stuck. | Flip the degree down to 3, then 3-1 collapse. Mean degree 6 is below the ear-queue crossover of about 9. Never gets stuck. | Write-min claims on the star plus a one-ring buffer, hash-of-ID priority; one child per parent per round. | Volume differences of touched cells. | Low; a few flips per operation. |
| **3D** | Locate in parent's star; 1-4 split; existing vote-locked 2-3/3-2 repair. The local Bowyer-Watson fallback handles the rare stuck facet. | Independent set with a one-ring buffer; per-victim conflict-only DT(link) under the same index-keyed SoS, then sew. Flip-down only as a later optimisation, with rebuild fallback. | Same claims; fixed round budget; defer losers to the next step. | Volume differences of touched cells. | Estimated 1–10 M operations/s for removal; unmeasured, must be benchmarked. |

The 1D column is our inference: no cited source treats 1D (de)refinement, and AREPO compiles it out. In 1D the "Delaunay triangulation" is the sorted order, every predicate is an exact integer comparison, and a split or merge touches at most three intervals. No flip machinery belongs there.

In 2D the whole design lives inside the existing flip kernel. Insertion and removal both reduce to local flips that never get stuck, which is the regime gDel2D exploits ([Cao et al. 2014](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/gDel3D.pdf)). In 3D, insertion stays in the flip kernel and removal gets one new kernel: a small per-victim rebuild that never stalls and needs only orient3d and insphere ([Buchin et al. 2013](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/mulzer/pubs/dtDel3DESA.pdf)). The schedule for both is to run derefinement first, then refinement, AREPO's order, on the already-repaired mesh at fixed positions ([AREPO public source](https://gitlab.mpcdf.mpg.de/vrs/arepo)). Feed the new facets into the step's normal flip rounds instead of a separate repair loop, and capture the fixed sequence of claim, split and rebuild kernels in a CUDA graph.

The benchmark that decides whether this "does not slow steps" should be stated in operations per second against the 5–11 M operations/s bar. It should be measured on uniform, jittered and clustered inputs, because gDel3D's clustered collapse shows that refinement hot spots, not the mean, set the cost ([Taveira et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06408v1)).

## Conclusion

Exact symbolic perturbation keyed to particle identity, which your mesh has by design, is the property that makes the hard case tractable. It is what Devillers and Teillaud had to engineer into CGAL to make 3D removal robust. Here it comes for free, and it turns removal into an embarrassingly parallel set of tiny independent rebuilds instead of a delicate flip sequence. Persistence turns the conservative remap into bookkeeping of volume differences. Together these make a persistent-mesh code structurally better placed for (de)refinement than AREPO, which pays for auxiliary meshes and clipping because it rebuilds every step.

The open risk is empirical, not algorithmic. Nobody has published GPU incremental insertion or removal rates, so the first measurement of the 3D removal kernel and of stuck-flip frequency for 1% near-parent batches will be a new data point. The physics' tolerance for deferral, cells staying off-threshold for a step or two, is the lever that keeps a bad tail from ever becoming a slow step.
