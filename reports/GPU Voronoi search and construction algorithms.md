# Stop rediscovering Voronoi topology every step

**For the per-step problem, the neighbour search and the clipping reconstruction are both near the best the literature offers. Doing that problem every step is the mistake.** Our nearest-first grid walk with a 2R security stop tests exactly the points the certificate requires and no more: 24–44 candidates and 75–115 grid cells per cell, matching a Monte Carlo prediction for glass-like to Poisson inputs. Its stop rule is stricter than that of any published GPU method. Our exact double-precision clipping kernel (3–11 M cells/s on an A100) is level with the fastest published GPU Voronoi builder, which runs at 9.3–9.5 M cells/s in fp32 on newer H200/RTX 5090 hardware and does not guarantee correct output. The new number that changes the strategy is our own: **computing face geometry from a known topology runs at ~300 M cells/s, 25–100× faster than discovering the topology by clipping.** At a 100 M cells/s full-step target, a step on the A100 has 10 ns per cell. Full re-clipping costs 90–330 ns per cell today and at least ~19 ns even at the A100's instruction-issue limit, so **a rebuild-every-step design cannot reach 100 M/s however well it is tuned.** The design that can is a maintained topology, checked each step with exact local Delaunay tests, with only the few cells that fail sent back to clipping. That design is not published for 3D GPU hydro. Its feasibility rests on a quantity nobody has measured: the fraction of cells whose topology actually changes in one hydro timestep. Our own code can measure it this week.

## Our kernel already matches the best published GPU builders

Once the numbers are taken from the right tables, the published state of the art is modest. Taveira et al. (SIGGRAPH 2026, "paragram") is the verified leader for plain 3D Voronoi. It takes **1.064 s for 10M and 1.582 s for 15M uniform points on an H200, and 1.078 s for 10M on an RTX 5090**, which is 9.3–9.5 M cells/s end-to-end. It runs in single precision with `-use_fast_math`, outputs adjacency only, and mismatches 0.1–0.2% of adjacencies against CGAL on real data ([Taveira et al., Table 7 and App. D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). At 10–15M points this GPU method is only **about 1.4–1.5× faster than 16-core HXT on a CPU** (1.064 vs 1.545 s at 10M; 1.582 vs 2.259 s at 15M) ([Taveira et al., Table 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). Xiao, Cao & Chen report **10.2 M/s on white noise and 19.2 M/s on blue noise** on an RTX 3080 in fp32. Those figures include construction and centroids but exclude allocation and transfer, and "exact" in that paper means combinatorial completeness, not exact arithmetic ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07175)). The only other FP64 GPU clipper, peclet-voro, reports 3.6 M cells/s for a cold build on an RTX 5080 and 8.5 M/s for construction from cached neighbours. These figures come from the repository's own report, not a peer-reviewed paper ([peclet-voro performance report](https://github.com/computational-chemical-engineering/peclet-voro/blob/main/docs/performance_report.md)). Global GPU Delaunay (gDel3D, double) runs at 3.4–4.7 M points/s and runs out of memory at ≥10M points on modern cards ([Taveira et al., Tables 1 and 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). votess on an A100 manages about 0.6 M/s, slower than 72-core CGAL ([votess JOSS plot](https://github.com/samridh-dev/votess/blob/main/joss/loglog.png)).

The same-GPU comparisons pin down where we stand. On our A100, our clipping is **2.7–4× faster than ProteusGPU's mesh construction**. ProteusGPU is the one public GPU moving-mesh hydro code, and its full Sedov step on 2.1M cells takes 0.422 s, or **0.005 G cell updates/s**, with 75% of the step spent on the mesh. At 7.4M cells it does about 5.2 M updates/s. On the same card, our fixed-grid JAX code LUNA does a full FP64 3D hydro step at **716 M cell updates/s at 128³ and 1,104 M at 256³**. A moving-mesh code is therefore 140–220× behind a Cartesian one on identical hardware. The 100 M/s target sits between them, roughly 7–11× below LUNA. Elsewhere, Cartesian GPU hydro exceeds 1e9 cell updates/s on one GH200 ([AthenaK, Stone et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16053)).

Several earlier claims in our notes do not survive checking and are not used here. These include the "8 M/s" paragram figure (a power-diagram table), a 12.5 M/s V100 rate for Ray et al. 2018 (an unverifiable snippet), a 33 M/s rate for Basselin et al. (contradicted by the authors' own slides, which give about 4.3 M seeds/s, [Basselin D1-Days slides](https://members.loria.fr/Olivier.Devillers/seminaires/Slides/2021-D1-Days/Basselin.pdf)), and the description of paragram's termination as an exact certificate. The paragram code caps its traversal stack at 64 entries and **silently drops the farthest node on overflow** ([paragram source](https://github.com/zenseact/paragram)). With fp32 arithmetic and post-hoc symmetrisation on top, the fastest published method certifies nothing. Our walk does.

## Neighbour search verdict: already optimal for its certificate

**Verdict: the search is the right design for near-uniform hydro meshes, and it is already at the floor that its stopping rule permits.** A nearest-first walk that stops when the next candidate is farther than 2R_current cannot stop before all natural neighbours are clipped. Once they are, R_current equals the final R_max. The walk therefore tests exactly the points inside B(g, 2R_max), a number fixed by the point set rather than by the implementation. Our Monte Carlo predicts **42.8 candidates in 99.8 unit grid cells for Poisson points and 23.2 in 72.5 for glass-like jitter**, against 15.56 true faces. Our measured 24–44 candidates and 75–115 grid cells fall exactly in that band. The face count agrees with the exact Poisson-Voronoi mean of 2 + 48π²/35 ≈ 15.535 ([Lazar et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.1736)). With 16.5–17.5 cuts per cell against about 15.5 faces, only about one cut per cell is wasted. Penetration-depth ordering or Xiao-style edge-driven bisector search can save at most that one cut. Xiao's method buys it with about 20 nearest-site queries per cell, a count reported for the serial variant; the parallel variant needs more ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07175)).

The alternatives are worse for our inputs. A fixed-k kNN that certifies cells needs k ≥ N(B(2R_max)), about 61 at p90 and 105 at p99.9 for Poisson points. That explains why Ray-derived codes ship with K = 90 for white noise and why Basselin's design fails on every clustered input in Taveira's tests ([Taveira et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). A BVH with best-first traversal earns its 27–37× advantage over depth-first only on clustered and real-world data. On Poisson input the gain is 1.30×, and directional culling adds 1.26–1.40× ([Taveira et al., Table 12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). RT-core and k-d-tree kNN reach 10⁸–10⁹ queries/s only with small fixed radii ([Wald 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.12859); [RTNN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.01366)). Voronoi search needs the opposite: an adaptive, shrinking bound delivered in nearest-first order.

Three improvements are justified. First, **directional (per-octant) grid-cell culling**. The union of the final vertex spheres covers only 37–53% of B(2R_max) and touches about 50 rather than 100 grid cells. Paragram's cheaper octant proxy is worth 1.25–1.44× on Poisson input. Second, **Morton-ordered threads**, which in RTNN were worth 4–6× for search coherence alone ([RTNN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.01366)). Third, **fusing or streaming the search instead of writing a candidate table**. Our current pipeline reserves 250 candidates per particle (3,000 B/particle of distances and IDs) to deliver a few dozen useful ones. That table is why 256³ does not fit on a 40 GB card. At 195³ the separate search costs 315 ms of a 1,211 ms pipeline, about 6.1 M particles/s overall (our job 31601918). A BVH is worth adding only for cosmological density contrasts. No published Voronoi benchmark characterises contrasts of 10³–10⁶, so robustness there is unproven for every method.

The deeper point is that **no search tuning shortens the walk, because the walk length is set by the certificate.** Warm-starting from the previous step's neighbours gives a nearly final cell after about 16 cuts, but the 2R walk must still cover B(2R_max). A warm start saves transient cuts, of which we have about one, and not search. The only way to remove the search is to change the certificate. That is the topic of the next section.

## Reconstruction verdict: right algorithm, wrong cadence

**Verdict: per-cell clipping is the correct algorithm for building a mesh from scratch, and our implementation is at the published frontier. Using it to rebuild every cell every step is what puts 100 M/s out of reach.** All competitive GPU Voronoi codes clip a small dual-triangle polytope per thread, with vertices stored as triples of plane indices. Paragram puts 96.2% of its fused kernel into clipping ([Taveira et al., App. D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). Star-splaying and lifted-star methods do the same work per cell with harder predicates. Nanjappa called gStar4D "enormously wasteful" because every tetrahedron is built in all four of its stars, and it lost to flip-based gFlip3D ([Nanjappa thesis](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/AshwinNanjappaThesis.pdf)). Local DeWall's empty-sphere walking claims a 2× speedup and 1.2 billion points on one GPU, but its baseline, precision and throughput are not accessible, so it cannot be ranked ([Gao & Chen, CAD 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448526000771)).

A first-principles budget on the A100 shows the limit. With 108 SMs, 4 schedulers per SM and 1.41 GHz, peak issue is about 6.1×10¹¹ warp instructions/s, or 1.95×10¹³ lane slots/s (our arithmetic from spec figures). At 100k thread instructions per cell and 8.7 of 32 lanes active, each cell consumes about 368k lane slots. **The issue rate alone therefore caps this kernel at about 53 M cells/s**, even with zero stalls. Measured throughput is 3–11 M/s, 6–21% of that cap, which fits the ~6% occupancy we see: a 1.2 KB shared-memory workspace allows about 136 threads per SM. The kernel is latency-bound before it is issue-bound. Reaching 100 M/s by clipping alone, at a realistic 60% of peak issue, would need about 117k lane slots per cell. That means roughly halving instructions *and* reaching about 25/32 active lanes *and* fixing occupancy, all at once. No published kernel reports even one of these numbers.

The face-parallel geometry pass shows what the same hardware does when the work is regular. It runs one thread per face, about 8 owned faces per cell, with circumcentres computed from a ring of about 5.2 entries, in double precision, agreeing with the clipping build to about 1e-13. It processes **2.1M cells in about 7 ms and 4.1M in about 13 ms**. Geometry is not the hard part. The cost lies in discovering the topology, and in a moving-mesh step most of that discovery repeats the previous step's result. The literature has hinted at this split: paragram outputs topology only, peclet-voro computes geometry in a separate per-vertex scatter, and gDel3D and Radiant Foam produce topology first. None of them measures how large the gap is, and our 25–100× ratio appears to be the first such measurement.

## Five directions ranked by distance to the A100's limits

The ranking orders directions by how much of the 10 ns/cell budget each can plausibly recover, weighted by evidence. The first two directions change what is computed. The last three change how efficiently the remaining clipping runs.

### 1. Maintain topology and recompute only geometry

This is the only direction that changes the arithmetic of the problem rather than its constant factor. **Evidence for:** our measured 25–100× gap between geometry and discovery is the core argument. Moving-point updates beat rebuilds when motion is small. On the CPU, flip-based 3D Delaunay restoration is "about 20 times as fast as computing a new triangulation" in the ideal case ([Schaller & Meyer-Hermann](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/physics/0302018)). On GPUs, Radiant Foam maintains adjacency "by performing an incremental Delaunay triangulation" in custom CUDA kernels across optimiser steps ([Radiant Foam](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01157)). peclet-voro repairs 3D Voronoi cells on the GPU at **21.8 M cells/s and 6.5× rebuild speed at δ/h = 1e-4** ([performance report](https://github.com/computational-chemical-engineering/peclet-voro/blob/main/docs/performance_report.md)). TESS seeds each cell from neighbours of neighbours ([Duffell & MacFadyen](https://arxiv.org/html/1104.3562)), and cellGPU tests circumcircles on the GPU every step ([cellGPU](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1702.02939)).

**Evidence against:** peclet-voro's repair falls to **0.84–0.86× of rebuild speed at δ/h ≥ 1e-2**, where 71.5% of cells are flagged each step, and it shows worst-cell volume errors of up to ~8e-4 on the GPU in the δ/h ≈ 5e-4–5e-3 band ([performance report](https://github.com/computational-chemical-engineering/peclet-voro/blob/main/docs/performance_report.md)). A Courant-limited hydro step moves generators by roughly 0.03–0.3 of a spacing in active regions (our estimate), beyond that break-even. The CGAL group calls rebuilding "surprisingly a very viable option" unless points move only slightly ([Manhães de Castro et al.](https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/INRIA/inria-00413344)), and cellGPU's CPU repair degrades in fluid-like regimes.

The way through is to notice that **peclet-voro flags cells with a conservative Verlet-skin test, not a topology test**, and that its first, purely seed-local certificate missed about 0.1% of neighbour relations per step. A flag rate is an upper bound on topology change, not a measurement of it. The exact alternative is the classical Delaunay lemma, which gFlip3D and gDel3D's flipping relies on ([gDel3D](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/gDel3D.pdf)): if every tetrahedron keeps its orientation and every interior facet passes the local insphere test, the whole triangulation is Delaunay. This needs no security-radius search at all. Our geometry pass already walks each face's ring of Voronoi vertices, which are the Delaunay tetrahedra. Checking about 13 facets per cell with a filtered insphere can be fused into that pass for perhaps 1,000 FP64 operations per cell (our estimate), well under 1 ns. Cells that fail go to a compacted repair stage. The repair either re-clips seeded from old neighbours and their neighbours, then certifies locally with the same test, or flips facets in the gFlip3D style when no tetrahedron has inverted.

**Unpublished:** no peer-reviewed GPU 3D Voronoi topology-reuse result with timings exists, no GPU hydro code uses an exact Delaunay-lemma certificate, and **no paper measures how many cells change topology per hydro step.** That last number decides the whole design. Our code can measure it directly by diffing neighbour sets between consecutive steps of a Sedov or Kelvin–Helmholtz run.

### 2. Make every decision exact, cheaply

Our degeneracy suite (sc/bcc/fcc lattices, icosahedral shells, planes, near-coincident pairs, perturbations from 0 to 1e-6) breaks both tolerance-based double and float kernels. Exactness is therefore a requirement, not a refinement. Paragram's 0.1–0.2% adjacency mismatch would mean roughly a million inconsistent faces per rebuild of a 10⁸-cell mesh (our arithmetic, assuming about 8 faces per cell). The useful structural fact is that **the clipping decision and the flip decision are the same predicate**. A Voronoi vertex of g, a, b, c is cut by the bisector of g and d exactly when d lies inside the circumsphere of g, a, b, c, so the decision is the insphere sign, normalised by orient3d. A single exact insphere therefore serves the clipper, the certificate and the flip repair.

**Evidence for:** gDel3D splits each predicate into a fast filtered kernel and a separate exact kernel that runs on compacted, flagged items. With that split, its performance on degenerate thin-sphere and grid inputs stays "very close" to uniform ([gDel3D](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/gDel3D.pdf)). Menezes et al. use GPU interval filters with CPU rational fallback and report up to 289× (IMR 2019) and 1,936× (CAD 2022) on predicate evaluation ([Menezes et al. 2019](https://internationalmeshingroundtable.com/assets/papers/2019/14-Menezes.pdf)). Integer positions in the GADGET style are already implemented in our `relative_early_integer32` path ([GADGET-4 intposconvert](https://gitlab.mpcdf.mpg.de/vrs/gadget4/-/blob/master/src/data/intposconvert.h)). Insphere on b-bit coordinate differences needs about 5b+6 bits, so signed 128-bit integers cover local differences up to about 24 bits; orient3d needs about 3b+4 bits (our derivation).

**Evidence against:** gFlip3D's exact insphere needs "hundreds of registers" and up to 14,880 floats of local storage ([Nanjappa thesis](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/AshwinNanjappaThesis.pdf)). Insphere also falls near zero more often than orientation does ([Devillers & Preparata](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/9907028)). The exact path must therefore live in its own launch, never inline in the hot kernel.

**Unpublished:** no published GPU clipper uses exact predicates, and the GPU cost of 128-bit integer insphere has not been measured. Xiao's "exact" result is fp32 with integer tie flags, and Ray/Basselin relaunch flagged cells in double, which is not exact either.

### 3. Stage the work into dense queues

**Evidence for:** splitting a divergent megakernel into specialised kernels fed by compacted queues raised path-tracing lane utilisation from 23% to 53% and throughput by 36–221% on identical code ([Laine, Karras & Aila 2013](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/publications/laine2013hpg_paper.pdf)). Our 8.7/32 lanes (27%) is in the same regime. Stage splitting in GPU collision detection gave 1.31–2.73× ([Lehericey et al. 2015](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2015/52996/52996.pdf)). Persistent threads with an atomic work counter gave 1.5–2.2× ([Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)). Shader execution reordering recovered 14.7× end-to-end in a photon megakernel, mainly through active-lane recovery. That result is from the abstract only, and SER is an OptiX feature that plain CUDA/Kokkos kernels cannot use ([Galgoczi 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21396)).

**Evidence against:** rays carry about 200 B of state, while our cells carry 1.2 KB or more. Clipping is also the divergent stage itself, because cut counts vary, so a search/clip split does not remove the divergence unless clipping is chunked into N cuts per pass with re-queuing. Paragram keeps a fused kernel, and its K = 8 pre-pass "is not recovered" on the H200 ([Taveira et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)).

Staging ranks third because the topology-maintenance pipeline is naturally a wavefront: a dense certify-and-geometry pass, a compacted repair queue, then a compacted exact-predicate queue. That is the gDel3D pattern applied to moving meshes. **Unpublished:** no measurement of staging for Voronoi clipping exists.

### 4. Move per-cell state out of shared memory and shrink it

**Evidence for:** the 6% occupancy follows directly from 1.2 KB of shared memory per thread against 164 KB per SM. Paragram abandoned shared memory for lane-strided global SoA (about 2.6 KB per cell). Its authors say shared memory "severely limit[s]" occupancy. Paragram also packs vertices as 1-byte plane triples and compacts planes at 85% fill ([Taveira et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). SoA versus AoS for per-item state was worth +80% in path tracing ([Laine et al. 2013](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/publications/laine2013hpg_paper.pdf)), and cutting memory traffic gave 1.9–2.1× in incoherent ray traversal ([Ylitie et al. 2017](https://users.aalto.fi/~laines9/publications/ylitie2017hpg_paper.pdf)). Our unsorted-candidate experiment hit 118 planes and overflowed the 64-vertex workspace, so in-place compaction is needed regardless of where the state lives.

**Evidence against:** paragram gives no quantitative ablation. With 8.7 of 32 lanes at different slot indices, "coalesced" access degrades to scattered L1 sector hits. **Unpublished:** a shared-versus-global ablation for clipping. This direction is a reliable constant factor, probably 1.3–2× (our estimate). Under direction 1 it applies only to the repair fraction.

### 5. Share candidates across neighbouring cells

**Evidence for:** neighbouring cells' 2R balls overlap heavily. GROMACS cluster-pair lists share j-data across 8×4 clusters, prune 50–75% of pairs and reach close to 100 G interactions/s ([Páll et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.09167)). gStar4D's reciprocated working sets are the only Voronoi analogue ([Nanjappa thesis](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d_files/AshwinNanjappaThesis.pdf)).

**Evidence against:** this is the weakest direction. Per-ray traversal beat warp packets in every case Aila and Laine measured, by 1.1× for primary rays and about 2× for diffuse rays, because packets visit nodes their members do not need ([Aila & Laine 2009](https://users.aalto.fi/~laines9/publications/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)). Our own warp-per-cell variant cost about 16k warp instructions per cell, against roughly 11.5k for thread-per-cell (100k × 32/8.7 / 32). GROMACS's interaction is cheap and uniform, whereas each Voronoi candidate triggers a state-dependent clip.

At best, sharing saves part of the search share, not clipping. The defensible form is a block that cooperatively loads a Morton tile's union of candidates into shared memory while each thread still clips independently. **Unpublished:** candidate sharing in any GPU per-cell clipper. If direction 1 succeeds, the search largely disappears and this direction loses its target.

## Where 100 M cells/s full-step is and is not plausible

A 100 M cells/s full step means 10 ns per cell, or 21 ms per step at 2.1M cells and 41 ms at 4.1M. The table splits that budget using measured costs where they exist and labelled estimates where they do not.

| Component | ns per cell | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry from known topology | 3.2–3.3 | measured: 7 ms / 2.1M, 13 ms / 4.1M |
| Unstructured hydro update | ~2.5–4 | estimate: LUNA's 0.9–1.4 ns × about 8/3 more faces per cell, plus indirect access |
| Exact Delaunay-lemma certificate, fused into geometry | <1 | estimate: ~13 filtered insphere tests per cell |
| **Left for topology repair** | **~2–4** | difference |
| Full re-clip of every cell today | 90–330 | measured: 3–11 M/s |
| Full re-clip at the A100 issue cap | ~19 | arithmetic: 53 M/s |
| ProteusGPU full step (benchmark) | ~200 | measured: 0.005 G/s, 75% mesh |

**100 M/s is plausible** when four conditions hold together. Topology is maintained rather than rebuilt. Geometry and the exact certificate run as one dense pass. The unstructured hydro kernel stays within about 3–4× of LUNA's per-cell cost. And topology repair fits in the remaining 2–4 ns per cell. With repair by re-clipping at today's ~10 M/s, that last condition allows **about 2–4% of cells per step**, or 7–11% if directions 3 and 4 bring repair clipping to ~30 M/s. Repair by local flips would allow more. These conditions fit near-uniform and moderately clustered flows with subsonic or Courant-limited motion. They fit especially well under hierarchical local timestepping, where only active cells move at all.

**100 M/s is not plausible** in four cases. The first is any design that re-clips every cell every step: even a perfect kernel at the issue cap spends ~19 ns on the mesh alone, and no published kernel comes within 5× of that cap. The second is regimes in which most cells change neighbours every step. If true topology churn tracks peclet-voro's flag rates (47–72% at δ/h of 5e-3 to 1e-2), repair costs more than a rebuild. The third is tolerance-based arithmetic, which our degeneracy tests show fails. The fourth is memory designs built around a 250-candidate table, which caps the A100 near 8M cells.

The hydro side is a separate risk. LUNA shows FP64 hydro at 1.1 G updates/s on this card, but the non-mesh quarter of ProteusGPU's step alone runs at about 20 M updates/s. An unstructured hydro kernel at 250–400 M/s is a target, not a measurement. Moving to an H100 adds about 1.2× in SMs and doubles FP64 per SM, but the register file per SM is unchanged, so it helps a latency-bound kernel by roughly the SM-and-clock ratio rather than 3× ([NVIDIA Hopper blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/)).

## Conclusion

The literature has spent eight years making the per-cell rebuild faster and more robust. On uniform data it has stalled at about 10–20 M cells/s in fp32, and at scale it is only about 1.5× ahead of a 16-core CPU. Our exact kernel already sits on that plateau, and our measurement adds a fact the literature never isolated: geometry is two orders of magnitude cheaper than topology discovery. That shifts the question. It is no longer how to clip faster but how rarely we can afford to clip. The answer depends on a measurable quantity no paper reports: the per-step fraction of cells whose Delaunay neighbourhood actually changes, as opposed to the fraction a conservative skin flags. If that fraction is a few percent, 100 M/s full-step is within reach on an A100. If it is tens of percent, it is not, whatever the kernel does.

The most valuable next step is therefore a measurement, not an optimisation. Diff the exact neighbour sets between consecutive steps of representative hydro runs, then prototype the fused geometry-plus-exact-insphere certificate pass. Two parts of this design appear to have no published precedent: an exact, search-free certificate for a maintained 3D Voronoi mesh on a GPU, and the first measured topology-churn rate for moving-mesh hydro. Integer-coordinate predicates that serve clipping, certification and flips with one insphere are a natural fit for both. If the design works, the clipping kernel becomes a repair tool, and its remaining work on lanes, occupancy and instruction count matters only for the cells that fail certification.
