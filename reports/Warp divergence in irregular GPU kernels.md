# Cut candidate tests before fighting divergence

**None of the six textbook fixes for SIMT divergence has published evidence of a 2x gain on a kernel like ours. Our own new measurement points to a bigger lever outside the list.** Uniform random cells test 44.7 candidate planes against 23.7 for jittered cells, for almost the same number of cuts (17.7 vs 16.4). The warp kernel's slowdown from jittered to uniform input is 15/8 ≈ 1.88x, and the candidate ratio is 1.89x. That match is our inference, not a profile, but it says the warp kernel's cost scales with candidate tests, not with cuts or with divergence. The literature agrees on four points. First, regrouping idle lanes pays off by large factors only for long-tailed per-item loops, and the biggest measured case (16.5x) uses hardware reordering that the A100 lacks. Second, persistent threads now mostly remove launch overhead, not lane idleness. Third, wavefront splitting fails when per-item state is large next to per-stage work, and our 1.7 KB per cell is about 8x Laine's 212 B. Fourth, sub-warp teams beat both thread-per-item and warp-per-item in irregular graph work, but nobody has measured them on polyhedron clipping. The two routes most likely to give 2x are (a) cheaper rejection of non-cutting candidates, which has a hard ceiling of 2.5x on uniform input and 1.45x on jittered input and a published 1.25–1.44x for a related bound, and (b) 8- or 16-lane teams in the warp kernel, which could plausibly reach 2x on jittered input only if serial per-cell overhead dominates. For the lean kernel, regaining occupancy (from 7.8%) plus lane refill could give about 2x, but that would only bring it up to the warp kernel. A one-day CPU and profiling pass can decide among these before any rewrite.

## Our numbers say candidate tests, not cuts, set the cost

Start with what the kernel is limited by, because every fix in the literature attacks a specific loss. Memory is not the limit: DRAM traffic is below 1% and the L2 hit rate is 99%. The kernels are latency- and divergence-bound. Achieved occupancy is 7.8% (lean, shared-memory limited at about 1.7 KB per cell) and 16.7% (warp, 130 registers per thread). Active lanes per warp are 5.4 and 15.6 of 32, and issue utilisation is 19% and 42%.

The new CPU counts split the work as follows (131k points; per-cell means):

| Input | Faces | Cuts | Candidates tested | Non-cutting candidates | Grid cells visited | Warp kernel | Lean kernel |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jittered | 15.9 | 16.4 | 23.7 | 7.3 (31%) | 72.5 | ~15 M cells/s | ~8 M cells/s |
| Uniform random | 15.5 | 17.7 | 44.7 | 27.0 (60%) | 103.3 | ~8 M cells/s | ~3.5 M cells/s |
| Ratio uniform/jittered | 0.97 | 1.08 | **1.89** | 3.7 | 1.42 | **1.88** | **2.29** |

If runtime were dominated by cuts (the topology rewrite), uniform input would be about 8% slower, not 88%. Grid-cell visits alone would predict 1.42x. Only the candidate-test count tracks the warp kernel's slowdown. For the warp kernel, the most economical reading is that each candidate costs roughly the same whether it cuts or not. That would happen if the grid walk, the candidate fetch and the warp-wide plane test (a ballot over about 30 vertices) take most of the time, and the topology edit is comparatively cheap. This is inference from two data points and three unknowns. A phase-level profile (experiment 1 below) should confirm it before anyone builds on it.

The lean kernel loses an extra 1.22x beyond the candidate ratio (2.29 vs 1.89). The simplest explanation is the SIMT max-of-32 effect. A thread-per-cell warp runs until its slowest lane finishes, and uniform random cells vary more in candidate count than jittered ones. The warp kernel cannot show this effect, because each warp holds one cell. This distinction decides which fixes can help which kernel, and it is the thread running through the rest of this report.

The certificate explains the waste. A candidate p cuts the current cell only if it lies inside some vertex sphere B(v, |v|), with the site at the origin. The user's bound (a candidate at distance d can only remove vertices with |v| ≥ d/2) follows from this. Our earlier Monte Carlo showed that the union of the final vertex spheres covers only **37% of the 2R_max ball for Poisson points and 53% for glass-like points**, and touches about 50 rather than 100 unit grid cells. The observed cutting fractions (40% uniform, 69% jittered) fit that picture. Nearest-first order still cannot stop the walk earlier, because the farthest point of the union lies exactly at 2R_max. What a tighter test can do is skip the plane test for a candidate, and skip whole grid cells, without changing the stopping rule.

## Regrouping idle lanes wins big only where per-item loops have long tails

The strongest results in the literature share one mechanism: when some lanes finish early, the SIMD unit picks up new work for them. On a GTX 285, Aila and Laine found ray traversal "1.5–2.5X off from theoretical optimum", mostly because of "inefficiencies in hardware work distribution" in which "long-running rays or warps keep distribution slots hostage". Persistent threads that fetch work from a global atomic counter made packet traversal **1.5–2.2x faster** and brought it within 10–20% of the bound ([Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)).

That gain depended on a weakness in the block scheduler of the time. Modern GPUs dispatch a new CTA as soon as one retires, so the hostage effect should be much smaller now. This is our inference; we found no peer-reviewed A100 or H100 measurement that isolates it. The modern persistent-kernel results are large, but they come from somewhere else. Atos on a V100 reports geomean speedups of 2.1–3.44x and a 12.8x peak for BFS on road_usa. Those gains come mainly from the "small-frontier problem" of many tiny launches. They also cost registers: the persistent-warp kernel used 72 registers against 42 for the discrete-warp kernel, and occupancy fell from 62% to 43% ([Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)). The cleaner test of lane refill inside a warp is Aila and Laine's "replacing terminated rays". It raised SIMD efficiency, but they found "no parameter values… that yielded consistent performance improvements on GTX285", because memory coherence and overhead got worse ([Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)). Regenerating paths inside a megakernel "whenever more than half of the threads in a warp are idling" gave about **15%** ([Laine, Karras & Aila 2013](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2013-07_Megakernels-Considered-Harmful/laine2013hpg_paper.pdf)).

The large modern numbers come from workloads with long, unpredictable per-item loops. On a V100, Speculative Reconvergence lets a thread whose inner loop has finished pull its next outer iteration and rejoin the others (Loop Merge). It improved SIMT efficiency and speed by **10% to 3x** on Monte Carlo codes, and the authors note that "SIMT efficiency improvement serves roughly as an upper bound on speedup" ([Damani et al. 2020](https://d1qx31qr3h6wln.cloudfront.net/publications/speculativereconvergence.pdf)). Collaborative Context Collection stashes divergent threads' registers in shared memory and reports a 1.69x mean ([Khorasani et al. 2015](http://www.cs.ucr.edu/~gupta/research/Publications/Comp/micro-48.pdf)). Thread-data remapping reports up to 2.7x ([Lin et al. 2018](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/on-gpu-thread-data-remapping-for-branch-divergence-reduction/)). The record is a 2026 preprint on optical-photon Monte Carlo, where a few long-lived photons hold their warps. Shader Execution Reordering cut propagation cycles **16.55x**: 3.2x came from the launch changes SER requires and 5.1x from the reorder itself, attributed to "active lane recovery" ([Galgóczi 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21396)). SER reorders only on Ada and later and only inside ray-tracing pipelines. "On previous generation GPUs, it will behave as a no-op" ([NVIDIA SER whitepaper](https://developer.nvidia.cn/sites/default/files/akamai/gameworks/ser-whitepaper.pdf)). On the A100 the hardware does not help. Independent Thread Scheduling did not change the cost of divergence either: divergent paths still serialise linearly, T(k) ≈ s·k, from Pascal to Blackwell ([Dale 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23402)).

Hardware compaction proposals (thread block compaction at 22% average, intra-warp compaction at 20% average) exist only in simulation ([Fung & Aamodt 2011](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/aamodt/papers/wwlfung.hpca2011.pdf); [Vaidya et al. 2013](https://class.ece.iastate.edu/tyagi/cpre581/papers/ISCA13Divergence.pdf)). They are useful only as a sense of scale: generic divergence recovery tends to give tens of percent, and multiples appear only on extreme tails.

How long is our tail? The relevant quantity is the per-warp ratio of the maximum to the mean of cell cost over 32 consecutive cells. We have not measured it. The 1.22x excess slowdown of the lean kernel on uniform input suggests a ratio of roughly 1.2–1.5, which is far from photon-lifetime territory. This is the single most useful number we lack. Per-cell candidate counts from the CPU check give it directly.

## Scoring the six fixes against our two kernels

The table gives our estimate for each fix. In the evidence column, "direct" means a measurement on comparable work, "analogy" means a measurement on different work transferred by argument, and "inference" means reasoning with no measurement behind it. No GPU Voronoi or Delaunay paper we found reports lane utilisation or attacks divergence directly. The fastest published builder maps "one-to-one onto GPU threads" and treats occupancy, not divergence, as the problem ([Taveira et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06408)). Every number below for our kernels is therefore analogy or inference.

| Fix | Lean kernel (5.4/32 lanes) | Warp kernel (15.6/32 lanes) | Evidence type | Plausible gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Persistent threads, dynamic fetch | Warp-granular fetch: tail only. Per-lane refill attacks max-of-32. | Inter-cell imbalance only causes an SM-level tail, which CTA dispatch already handles. | Analogy (GTX 285, V100) | Lean: 1.1–1.4x with refill. Warp: 0–5%. |
| (2) Tail deferral, caps, big-capacity second pass | Smaller pass-1 state raises shared-memory-limited occupancy. Also trims the tail. | Little effect: occupancy is register-bound. | Analogy (SpMV, Hong) plus inference | Lean: 1.3–1.8x, mostly from occupancy. Warp: under 10%. |
| (3) Sub-warp teams of 4/8/16 | Replaces the kernel. | Amortises serial per-cell work over 2–4 cells per warp. | Analogy (BFS, hashing) | Warp: 1.2–2x, only if serial phases dominate. |
| (4) Stage splitting, wavefront queues | Per-cut splitting moves about 70 KB per cell. | Same. | Direct mechanism, analogy for size | Per-cut split: likely a slowdown. Coarse split is the same as (2). |
| (5) Sort cells by predicted cost | Cuts max-of-32. Locality loss is cheap with DRAM idle. | No lane-level effect. | Analogy (ray sorting) | Lean: up to the max/mean ratio, probably 1.1–1.3x. Warp: ~0. |
| (6) Branch-free formulations | Cuts divergence inside the cut code. | Inside the serial rewrite. | Weak analogy | 1.1–1.3x at best. |
| Extra: cheap rejection of non-cutting candidates | Fewer serial vertex loops per cell. | Fewer walk, fetch and plane-test rounds. | Direct (related bound in a Voronoi code) plus our counts | Uniform: 1.3–1.8x, ceiling 2.5x. Jittered: ceiling 1.45x. |

### Persistent threads help the lean kernel only through per-lane refill

Persistent threads fix idle slots at the scheduler level. They do nothing for idle lanes inside a running warp. Our warp-per-cell kernel puts one cell in each warp, so its 15.6/32 is caused by what happens inside a cell, not by imbalance between cells. Warp-granular fetching therefore has nothing to fix in that kernel, and Atos's register cost would hurt a kernel already at 130 registers ([Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)).

The lean kernel is different. The variant that matches its loss is a while-while loop in which a lane that certifies its cell immediately takes the next cell index. In practice: ballot the finished lanes, use one warp-aggregated atomic per refill, and re-initialise the cube in place. Aila and Laine's reasons for failure (memory incoherence) carry little weight for us, because the memory system is idle. Damani's Loop Merge is the right template, with the caveat that a soft threshold is needed so that the refill prologue does not itself run at low utilisation ([Damani et al. 2020](https://d1qx31qr3h6wln.cloudfront.net/publications/speculativereconvergence.pdf)). The ceiling is the per-warp max/mean ratio of cell cost, which we estimate at 1.2–1.5. Part of the lean kernel's 5.4/32 lanes is divergence inside each cut, which refill cannot touch.

### Tail deferral works through occupancy, not through the tail

Capacity-limited first passes with deferral are universal in GPU Voronoi and Delaunay codes. Basselin et al. relaunch with larger arrays ([Taveira et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06408)), votess falls back to the CPU ([Singh et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04514v2)), and gDel3D repairs on the CPU ([gDel3D](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gdel3d.html)). All of them use deferral for robustness, not for speed. The throughput evidence comes from SpMV. Routing very long rows to a dedicated kernel "effectively doubles" CSR-Adaptive on irregular matrices ([Daga & Greathouse 2015](https://hgpu.org/?p=14791)). Hong et al. found that deferring outliers helped on its own but gave "only a marginal performance gain" once virtual warps were in place ([Hong et al. 2011](https://ppl.stanford.edu/papers/ppopp070a-hong.pdf)).

Our cells are not power-law distributed: about 16 cuts with a modest spread. The tail-trimming part of (2) is therefore small. The real lever is the side effect on occupancy. The lean kernel is shared-memory limited at about 1.7 KB per cell, so a pass-1 capacity near the 90th-percentile cell, with overflow sent to the existing big-capacity pass, raises resident warps roughly in proportion. Taveira et al. went further and moved per-thread state to coalesced global memory, because the per-thread footprint "severely limit[ed] SM occupancy" ([Taveira et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06408)). With a 99% L2 hit rate, we can afford that trade.

One caution comes from our own data. Capping registers to fit 5 blocks per SM in the warp kernel gave only 4%, so extra occupancy does not automatically turn into throughput. The lean kernel at 7.8% occupancy and 19% issue utilisation has more headroom, but the gain is untested.

### Sub-warp teams are the only listed fix that targets the warp kernel's loss

Hong et al.'s virtual warps of 4, 8 or 16 lanes "trade off execution-path divergence and ALU underutilization". On regular graphs, utilisation peaked when the team size was close to the average degree. Full warps won only on the heavily skewed RMAT graph. They also identify the Amdahl limit of a full warp per task: the ratio of SIMD to SISD phase duration caps the gain ([Hong et al. 2011](https://ppl.stanford.edu/papers/ppopp070a-hong.pdf)). Hash tables and filters land on sub-warp tiles as well: "for the majority of the configurations, this size is 4" ([McCoy et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09005)). WarpCore uses tiles of 1–32 lanes with a vote-and-elect-leader pattern for the serial mutation ([Jünger et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.07914)).

For us, the plane test over about 30 vertices is the SIMD phase. Everything else is effectively SISD: the grid walk, candidate fetch, stopping test, and the walk around the new face (about 5–7 edges). Running that serial part in the warp kernel idles 31 lanes. Four 8-lane teams run four cells' serial work in the same instructions, provided their control flow agrees.

Two facts limit this. First, if the candidate loop dominates, as our counts suggest, then the serial share includes the walk and fetch, which helps the case for teams. Second, the four teams diverge whenever one cell's candidate cuts and another's does not, which on uniform input happens 60% of the time. The standard mitigation evaluates the cheap test for all teams first and then runs the expensive rewrite once, with every team that needs it active together. A 2x gain needs the serial share above about 60%, and that has to be profiled. Kokkos `TeamPolicy` with a vector length of 8 or 16 maps onto this directly.

### Stage splitting fails on state size

Wavefront path tracing raised thread utilisation from 23% to 53% and was 36% faster even in a simple scene. It worked because each stage carried heavy work against only 212 B of SOA path state, and because register-lean ray-cast kernels hid latency better. Ray casts nevertheless stayed at 35% utilisation and took 44–56% of the time ([Laine, Karras & Aila 2013](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2013-07_Megakernels-Considered-Harmful/laine2013hpg_paper.pdf)). A 2026 rerun on an RTX 3060 Ti found only 16% ([Padilla et al. 2026, preprint](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2605.27323v1)). NVIDIA's own advice is that reordering costs grow with live state and that it should not be used before trivial work ([NVIDIA SER whitepaper](https://developer.nvidia.cn/sites/default/files/akamai/gameworks/ser-whitepaper.pdf)).

Splitting our kernel per cut would move about 3.4 KB of state per round trip, or roughly 70 KB per cell. At 1.5–2 TB/s, that caps throughput near 2–3 × 10⁷ cells/s and turns a latency-bound kernel into a bandwidth-bound one. This is our arithmetic. A coarse split with one queue hand-off per cell is affordable, but it is option (2) under another name.

### Cost sorting is capped by a variance we have not measured

Ray reordering speeds up RTX trace kernels by 1.3–2.0x, but "recovering the reordering overhead… is problematic" ([Meister et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11273v1)). Gains of 2x or more from binning appear only for power-law SpMV against weak baselines ([Greathouse & Daga 2014](https://core.ac.uk/works/100827075); [Hou et al. 2017](https://seec.cs.vt.edu/pubs/papers/hou-spmv-ashes17.pdf)). Barnes-Hut sorts bodies spatially and takes warp-wide votes on the opening test, trading wasted work for uniform control flow ([Burtscher & Pingali 2011](https://userweb.cs.txstate.edu/~mb92/papers/gcg11.pdf)).

Our Morton experiment (±1%) shows that locality is not the issue. A sort by predicted cost (local density, or last step's candidate count in a moving-mesh code) can only reduce the per-warp max/mean ratio in the lean kernel. It does nothing in the warp kernel. At 65–125 ns per cell, a full radix sort per pass must also be cheap enough to pay for itself.

### Branch-free code reshapes small branches, not trip counts

We found no measurement of predicated against branchy polyhedron clipping. Branch distribution (factoring shared code out of both sides of a branch) gave up to 80% on synthetic kernels but 16% on a real application ([Han & Abdelrahman 2011](https://www.eecis.udel.edu/~cavazos/cisc879-spring2012/papers/a3-han.pdf)). Full unrolling also blocks Loop Merge and raises register use ([Damani et al. 2020](https://d1qx31qr3h6wln.cloudfront.net/publications/speculativereconvergence.pdf)). Our dominant variable is the number of candidates per cell, which no predication removes. A fixed-K candidate loop would waste work on cells that certify early and still fail on cells that need more than K.

## Directional bounds are the best-evidenced lever, outside the list of six

The one published measurement close to the user's suggested lever comes from the fastest GPU Voronoi code itself. Taveira et al. cull candidates and BVH nodes when the bisector distance exceeds a directional radius, taken per octant from the corners of the cell's bounding box. Replacing that with a single isotropic radius, which is exactly our 2R ball, makes the full pipeline **1.33x slower on Poisson input (range 1.25–1.44)**, 1.26x on clustered input and 1.40x on real-world input, on an RTX 5090 ([Taveira et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06408)). That is direct evidence on the same problem class. It is end-to-end and measured in FP32 on a different GPU, so it transfers as a range, not a number.

Our counts set the ceiling. If cost scales with candidates, rejecting every non-cutting candidate for free would give 44.7/17.7 ≈ **2.5x on uniform input** and 23.7/16.4 ≈ **1.45x on jittered input**. Reaching the ceiling is impossible, because the exact test (is the candidate inside some vertex sphere?) is the plane test. What is buyable is a cheap conservative pre-test, and there are three of increasing cost:

1. **Per-octant maximum vertex radius**, R_oct[8], updated after each cut. A candidate in direction u at distance d is skipped if d/2 exceeds the largest R_oct over the octants its bisector can reach. Grid cells are skipped by the same rule. This is close to the published scheme.
2. **A k-DOP of support values** h_k = max_v v·n_k over 13 or 26 fixed directions. Its bound on max_v v·u is tighter, at roughly 13–26 extra FMAs per cut.
3. **The user's |v| ≥ d/2 filter**, used inside the plane test rather than before it. Keep vertices bucketed by radius, and for far candidates scan only the few vertices that can be removed.

The first two cut the number of candidate rounds. The third cuts the cost of each round, which matters most in the lean kernel's serial vertex loop.

There are two reasons to expect less than the final-cell figures suggest. During construction the vertex spheres are larger than at the end, so early candidates are harder to reject. And in the warp kernel a skipped candidate saves a ballot round, but not the grid walk that found it, unless grid cells are culled too. A realistic target is 1.3–1.8x on uniform input and 1.1–1.3x on jittered input. That makes this the only lever with direct evidence that also attacks the measured 1.89x gap.

## Conclusion

The question was framed as a divergence problem, but the data reframe it as a work problem with a divergence surcharge. In the warp kernel, divergence is internal to each cell and stays the same across inputs; the uniform-input penalty is almost exactly the extra candidate tests. In the lean kernel, divergence adds about 1.2x on top of that. This means the scheduling fixes (1), (2) and (5) can at most close the lean kernel's gap to the warp kernel. They cannot lift the warp kernel. The only paths to 2x over today's best kernel run through doing less work per cell (directional rejection) or through amortising serial per-cell work across lanes (sub-warp teams). The two stack, because rejection shortens the serial candidate loop that teams would share.

The evidence base is thin in a way worth noting. No published Voronoi or Delaunay GPU code reports lane utilisation, so our 5.4/32 and 15.6/32, together with candidate counts, are new data for this literature. Nearly every multiple-x number cited above comes from ray tracing, graphs or SpMV.
