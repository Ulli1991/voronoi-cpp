## Q1. What speedups do persistent threads / dynamic work fetching, tail deferral, and cost sorting/binning give on long-tail workloads, and do Fermi/Kepler-era gains persist on Volta/A100/Hopper?

### Takeaway
The best-documented measured gain from persistent threads with a global atomic work counter is 1.5–2.2x for packet ray traversal on a GTX 285 (Tesla generation). The mechanism was a hardware block scheduler that let long-running warps "keep distribution slots hostage". Later evidence on V100 (Atos) shows persistent kernels still give large gains (geomean 2.1–3.44x, peak 12.8x). Those gains come mostly from removing many small launches and from asynchronous task parallelism, not from per-item tail balancing. Per-thread refill of finished lanes ("replacing terminated rays") improved SIMD efficiency in simulation but gave no consistent speedup on real hardware. I found no peer-reviewed A100/H100 measurement that isolates per-item tail balancing by persistent threads.

### Cited Findings
**Persistent threads / dynamic fetch: mechanism and numbers**
- Aila & Laine found earlier GPU ray-traversal kernels to be "a factor of 1.5–2.5X off from theoretical optimum". They write that "most of the gap is not explained by memory bandwidth, but rather by previously unidentified inefficiencies in hardware work distribution." Hardware: NVIDIA GTX 285, 1024x768. — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- Mechanism, quoted: CUDA work distribution units "are optimized for homogeneous units of work… in ray tracing the execution time of individual rays vary wildly, and that may cause starvation issues in work distribution when long-running rays or warps keep distribution slots hostage." Their fix was "launching only enough threads to fill the machine once. These long-running persistent threads can then fetch work from a global pool using an atomic counter until the pool is empty." — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- Result: "the packet traversal got 1.5–2.2 times faster, and its performance is now within 10–20% of the theoretical upper bound for all ray types". The persistent while-while kernel "shows remarkably similar development". Fetching is per warp, from a global counter, in batches. — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- Before introducing persistent threads, they found that the if-if loop organisation beat while-while partly because "the slowest warps are about 30% faster in if-if". In other words, the length of the tail of warp execution times mattered more than average SIMD efficiency, because of the slot-hostage effect. — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- The Kepler/Fermi addendum (2012) reports that ray tracing remains "almost entirely limited by the available FLOPS" on those generations. Its abstract does not restate the persistent-thread gain, and I could not extract its body text. — [Aila, Laine, Karras 2012](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2012-06_understanding-efficiency-ray-traversal-gpus-kepler-and-fermi-addendum)
- Gupta, Stuart & Owens (abstract): persistent threads can give "up to an order-of-magnitude speedup over nonPT kernels, but can also result in performance loss in many cases". They name four use cases: CPU–GPU synchronization, load balancing/irregular parallelism, producer–consumer locality, and global synchronization. — [Gupta et al. 2012](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3j76d3td)

**Modern GPU (V100) persistent-kernel evidence**
- Atos (ICPP 2022) ran on a V100 32 GB with CUDA 11.1. Against BSP baselines (Gunrock for BFS and PageRank, custom BSP for coloring), it reports geomean speedups of 3.44x (BFS), 2.1x (PageRank) and 2.77x (graph coloring), with peaks of 12.8x, 3.2x and 9.08x. — [Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)
- In Atos, the 12.8x peak is BFS on road_usa: 604 ms (Gunrock) vs 174 ms (discrete-kernel CTA) vs 46.9 ms (persistent CTA). The persistent-vs-discrete gain there comes mainly from the "small-frontier problem", where many launches each carry little work. — [Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)
- Atos costs: the persistent-warp kernel used 72 registers at 43% occupancy, vs 42 registers at 62% for discrete-warp. The atomics used for queue pops are an explicit cost. Some configurations were slower than BSP, for example discrete-warp graph coloring at 0.28x on hollywood-2009. — [Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)
- Atos: CTA-sized workers beat warp-sized workers on all BFS runs, which the authors attribute to lower per-worker workload variance, since CTA workers combine task-parallel and intra-CTA data-parallel balancing. — [Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)

**Divergence cost on post-Volta GPUs (relevant to "does ITS change the picture")**
- A 2026 preprint, not peer-reviewed, tested Pascal, Ampere, Hopper and Blackwell with microbenchmarks. Divergent paths serialize linearly, T(k) ≈ s·k, so warp execution efficiency falls as 32/k. The same cost model holds on Pascal, so Independent Thread Scheduling did not change the programmer-visible divergence cost. Only the reconvergence ISA mechanics changed (per-warp SSY/SYNC stack replaced by barrier registers). — [Dale 2026, arXiv:2607.23402](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23402)
- An octree gravity code on Volta reports that, under ITS, divergence can persist "until executing any explicit synchronization even after the conditional-branch ended", so explicit `__syncwarp` may be needed to restore reconvergence. — [arXiv:1811.02761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.02761)
- A UBC thesis (Vulkan-Sim) reportedly found that independent thread scheduling and function-call coalescing, "despite their SIMT efficiency benefits… provide limited performance benefits for ray tracing workloads". This is a secondary, search-snippet-level citation that I did not verify in full text. — [UBC thesis](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0448289)

**Tail deferral / multi-pass with caps**
- Aila & Laine "Replacing terminated rays": they "modified the persistent threads approach to periodically replace the terminated rays with new ones". They capped the inner while loops at N=4 iterations so a refilled lane can rejoin. Result: "This approach clearly improves the SIMD efficiency, but it also leads to less coherent memory accesses and causes computational overhead… no parameter values were found that yielded consistent performance improvements on GTX285. In some individual viewpoints ambient occlusion rays got almost 10% faster." They estimated the overhead could be halved by warp-wide prefix-sum/popcount instructions. Those instructions exist today as `__ballot_sync`/`__popc` and the cooperative groups API. — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- CSR-Adaptive's extension for irregular matrices is effectively length-based deferral to a different kernel: rows are routed by length to one of two algorithms, plus "a third algorithm for very long rows". The authors say this "effectively doubles" CSR-Adaptive's performance on irregular matrices (AMD GPUs). — [Daga & Greathouse 2015](https://hgpu.org/?p=14791)

**Sorting / binning by cost**
- Ray reordering on an RTX 2080 Ti (Turing) "yields significantly higher trace speed on recent GPUs (1.3–2.0x), but recovering the reordering overhead in the hardware-accelerated trace phase is problematic". Example, third bounce of a wavefront path tracer: 2355 MRays/s unsorted, 3593 MRays/s with origin-direction sort (1.53x), 3914 MRays/s with origin–termination-point sort (1.66x). — [Meister et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11273v1)
- Garanzha & Loop 2010: a sorted, breadth-first packet ray tracer pipeline was about 3x faster than an optimized single-kernel depth-first tracer on the same data. The 3x conflates sorting, frustum culling and breadth-first traversal, so it is not a sorting-only number. — [Garanzha & Loop 2010](https://diglib.eg.org/xmlui/handle/10.2312/CGF.v29i2pp289-298)
- CSR-Adaptive assigns variable numbers of rows to each workgroup by row length, a form of binning. It reports a 14.7x average speedup over existing CSR algorithms and 2.3x over the clSpMV "cocktail" format selector (AMD GPUs). The 14.7x baseline includes weak CSR-scalar/vector kernels, so it is not a pure binning gain. — [Greathouse & Daga 2014](https://core.ac.uk/works/100827075)
- Hou, Feng & Che (AMD Research) group rows into bins and auto-tune a kernel per bin, reporting 1.2x to 52.0x over the default SpMV across 16 matrices. — [Hou et al. 2017](https://seec.cs.vt.edu/pubs/papers/hou-spmv-ashes17.pdf)
- Merge-based SpMV splits the merged work sequence (row ends plus nonzeros) into exactly equal shares, so no sort is needed. On a K40m with circuit5M it ran 6.92 ms vs 316.8 ms for cuSPARSE CsrMV (about 46x), and was about 1.25x faster than cuSPARSE HybMV's kernel time, which also needs about 1 s of setup. The authors stress performance "uncorrelated to the distribution of nonzeros among rows". — [Merrill & Garland, merge-spmv repo](https://github.com/dumerrill/merge-spmv); [SC16 abstract](https://sc16.supercomputing.org/presentation/index-id=pap369&sess=sess147.html)
- The Loops library (PPoPP 2023) decouples load balancing from work processing and matches CUB's merge-based SpMV almost exactly across roughly all of SuiteSparse. — [Osama et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04792)

### Inferences
- Fermi/Kepler-era persistent-thread gains came from a specific hardware weakness: the block scheduler was coarse and fresh blocks could not fill slots held by long-running warps. Modern GPUs (Kepler onward) can dispatch a new CTA as soon as any CTA retires on an SM, so the "slot hostage" effect should be smaller. The large modern gains (Atos) come from removing launches and barriers, not from tail balancing within a single launch. So on an A100, expect gains from persistent threads alone to be well under the 1.5–2.2x GTX 285 figure for a single-launch kernel. This is not measured.
- The linear-serialization result (Dale 2026) means ITS does not rescue a warp whose lanes wait for the longest item. A warp still runs as long as its slowest lane, so lane-level waste must be fixed by regrouping work (sorting, binning, refill, deferral), not by the scheduler.
- Evidence for more than 2x from sorting or binning exists only where the baseline is pathologically imbalanced (SpMV with power-law rows, CSR-Adaptive 14.7x, Hou up to 52x) or where sorting is combined with an algorithm change (Garanzha 3x). Pure reordering of otherwise identical work gives about 1.3–2.0x (Meister).

### Gaps
- I could not extract Gupta et al. 2012's per-benchmark numbers or hardware (abstract only).
- I could not extract the Aila/Laine/Karras 2012 addendum's statement on whether persistent threads still help on Fermi/Kepler.
- I found no peer-reviewed measurement of persistent threads vs plain launch for a per-item, long-tailed, single-launch kernel on A100 or H100.

## Q2. When does persistent threading NOT help?

### Takeaway
Persistent threading fixes idle slots between work items at the scheduler level. It does nothing for idle lanes inside a warp that is still running (waiting for the slowest lane, or divergent control flow inside each item). It can hurt through extra registers (lower occupancy), atomic contention, lost memory coherence, and occasionally changed algorithmic behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- Per-lane refill, which targets intra-warp idleness, improved SIMD efficiency but gave "no consistent performance improvements on GTX285" and "hurts in cases where the SIMD efficiency is already high (primary rays)". The causes named were less coherent memory accesses and computational overhead. — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- Speculative traversal, which keeps idle lanes doing useful-if-maybe-wasted work, gave up to 5% on primary rays and up to 10% on AO, but no gain on diffuse rays: "the redundant node fetches start to hurt the performance… first clear sign of trace() being limited by memory bandwidth." — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)
- Persistent threads "can also result in performance loss in many cases" (abstract). — [Gupta et al. 2012](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3j76d3td)
- Atos: the persistent-warp kernel needs 72 vs 42 registers, cutting occupancy from 62% to 43%. The authors recommend persistent kernels mainly when the small-frontier/launch-overhead problem is present. Otherwise discrete kernels may be preferable. On scale-free graph coloring, persist-CTA's extra conflicts outweighed its concurrency gains. — [Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)
- The atomic counter must not "cause significant serialization". Aila & Laine fetch per warp in batches for this reason. — [Aila & Laine 2009](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2009-08_Understanding-the-Efficiency/aila2009hpg_paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- In our kernel, active threads per warp are 5.4/32 (thread-per-cell) and 15.6/32 (warp-per-cell). That is an intra-warp problem. Persistent CTAs or warps fetching new batches only address the SM-level tail. At 8–17% occupancy, an SM holds few warps, so a single long warp near the end of the grid can leave the SM nearly idle. That end-of-grid tail is small relative to total runtime when there are many cells (millions) per launch. I therefore expect warp-granular persistent fetching alone to give roughly 0–15% for us, not 2x. This is inference.
- Per-lane refill (each lane takes a new cell when its cell finishes) does target our 5.4/32 problem. The Aila & Laine reasons it failed matter less for us, because our memory system is idle (DRAM <1%, L2 hit 99%), so incoherence after refill is cheap. Per-cell state lives in a per-thread shared-memory slot, so re-initialising a cube is cheap. The remaining cost is control-flow restructuring: one loop iteration = one plane cut, plus an "if done, fetch next cell" step. That puts a fetch on the critical path of every iteration and reintroduces divergence between init/fetch code and the cut code. Synchronous refill via `__ballot_sync` plus an atomic per batch of finished lanes reduces atomic traffic.
- Warp-per-cell mode (15.6/32 active) has divergence inside each cut (variable vertex and edge counts per polyhedron). Neither persistent threads nor sorting fixes that. It is an algorithmic or data-layout issue.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures per-lane refill ("ray replacement") on Volta or later hardware with warp intrinsics.

## Q3. Evidence that sorting or binning by cost gives more than 2x, and how it applies here

### Takeaway
More than 2x from binning is well documented in irregular SpMV, but against weak baselines and on power-law row distributions. For coherent reordering of equal-algorithm work (ray sorting), measured gains are 1.3–2.0x in the trace kernel, before sort overhead. Cost-binning combined with per-bin specialized kernels or capacities, as in CSR-Adaptive's three-way length binning and Hou's per-bin auto-tuning, is the pattern most often reported at 2x or more.

### Cited Findings
- CSR-Adaptive (length-adaptive row grouping): 14.7x average over prior CSR kernels and 2.3x over clSpMV. The irregular-matrix extension (length-based routing to three kernels) "effectively doubles" performance. — [Greathouse & Daga 2014](https://core.ac.uk/works/100827075); [Daga & Greathouse 2015](https://hgpu.org/?p=14791)
- Binning plus a per-bin kernel: 1.2–52x across 16 matrices. — [Hou et al. 2017](https://seec.cs.vt.edu/pubs/papers/hou-spmv-ashes17.pdf)
- Ray reordering on RTX 2080 Ti: 1.3–2.0x trace speed, but sort overhead is "problematic" to recover once traversal is hardware-accelerated. The termination-point-estimate key (a predicted-cost/locality key) beat the plain origin-direction key: 3914 vs 3593 MRays/s. — [Meister et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11273v1)
- Ray sorting plus breadth-first pipeline: about 3x vs depth-first single kernel. — [Garanzha & Loop 2010](https://diglib.eg.org/xmlui/handle/10.2312/CGF.v29i2pp289-298)
- Merge-path (perfect static partition, no sort) makes runtime insensitive to the row-length distribution: about 46x over cuSPARSE CsrMV on circuit5M (K40m). This alternative needs work to be splittable at fine grain. — [merge-spmv](https://github.com/dumerrill/merge-spmv)
- Atos: CTA workers (lower per-worker variance) beat warp workers on all BFS runs. Grouping work to reduce variance per execution unit pays off. — [Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.00132)

### Inferences (applicability to our Voronoi kernel)
- Our upper bound is set by data: jittered points run 2x faster than uniform random. If the gap is due to a long tail of large cells, perfect scheduling cannot make random inputs faster than (total work)/(peak per-lane throughput). Jittered timing is a rough empirical ceiling, so realistic scheduling gains on random input are at most about 2x, and the full 2x only if total work is similar. Compare total cut counts (sum over cells) between jittered and random to separate "more work" from "badly distributed work". This is untested.
- **Sorting by predicted cost (most promising cheap step).** A cheap per-cell cost proxy exists before clipping: distance to the k-th nearest neighbour, the number of candidates in the cell-list stencil, or the cut count from the previous timestep in a moving-mesh code. Radix-sort the cell indices by (cost bin, Morton code), so warps hold similar-cost cells and keep spatial locality inside a bin. This attacks the 5.4/32 active-lane problem directly, which persistent threads do not. By analogy with ray sorting (1.3–2.0x) and since our memory is idle (low cost of losing locality), 1.3–2x is plausible on uniform random points. The sort is a few ms per 10^7 keys on A100, so check it against kernel time.
- **Tail deferral with capacity caps (most promising for 2x+).** Run pass 1 with a reduced shared-memory capacity per cell (for example, a vertex cap near the 90th-percentile cell size) and a cut-count cap. Cells that overflow or exceed the cap are compacted, via atomic append or `__ballot_sync` aggregation, into a queue. Pass 2 handles them warp-per-cell or with large scratch; the existing "rebuild overflowing cells with big capacities" path (commit 0a53f4b) is already this pattern. Two effects stack. (a) Thread-per-cell occupancy is shared-memory-limited at 8%, and smaller per-cell scratch raises occupancy proportionally. (b) Removing tail cells from pass-1 warps cuts lane idle time. This resembles CSR-Adaptive's routing of long rows to a dedicated kernel ("effectively doubles"). The risk is that pass-2 work is irregular too, but it is a small fraction of the cells.
- **Persistent threads.** Warp-granular persistent fetching is low priority (see Q2). Per-lane refill inside thread-per-cell is the variant matching our bottleneck. Expected gain is uncertain. Aila & Laine saw no consistent gain on GTX 285, but their failure modes (memory incoherence) are weak in our case. Try it only after sorting and deferral, because sorting already reduces intra-warp variance and makes refill less valuable.
- **Task runtimes (CDP, Whippletree, Softshell, Atos).** These are designed for dynamic work generation and dependencies. Our cells are independent and known up front, so a single global queue or a sorted index list captures the benefit without a runtime. Atos's own data shows the persistent-kernel register overhead (42 to 72 registers) would hurt our already register-limited warp-per-cell kernel.

### Gaps
- Whippletree and Softshell report "significantly"/"substantially better" performance than kernel-based approaches (Reyes rendering, procedural geometry, volume rendering) in their abstracts. I could not extract numeric speedups. — [Whippletree](https://arbook.icg.tugraz.at/schmalstieg/Schmalstieg_286.pdf); [Softshell](https://arbook.icg.tugraz.at/schmalstieg/Schmalstieg_247.pdf)
- I did not retrieve measured CUDA Dynamic Parallelism launch overhead numbers. From prior knowledge, studies such as Wang & Yalamanchili (IISWC 2014) report high device-launch overheads for fine-grained child kernels. That is unverified in this session, so do not cite it without checking.
- I found no paper that sorts geometric-construction work items (Voronoi/Delaunay cells) by predicted cost on GPUs. The applicability judgements above are analogies.
